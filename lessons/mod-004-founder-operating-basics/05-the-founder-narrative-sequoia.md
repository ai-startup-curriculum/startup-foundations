# The Founder-Narrative in the Sequoia Shape

## Motivation

The operating loop from chapter 01 has a communicate step. Chapter
02's Monday plan and Friday shipped-and-learned handle the *weekly*
communication to the co-founder and the team. Chapter 06's investor
update handles the *monthly* communication to investors. This
chapter handles the third and largest communication artifact — the
**founder-narrative** — the version of the company that a
co-founder, a first employee, a first customer, and a first
investor can each act on.

The narrative is not the pitch deck. Pitch decks are a
consequence of the narrative. It is not the marketing site. The
marketing site is a consequence too. It is not the vision talk at
the offsite; that is a consequence as well. The narrative is the
underlying *story* — ten specific parts, in a specific order —
from which each of those consequences is derived. Author the
narrative once, correctly; the derivatives cost hours instead of
weeks.

This chapter installs the ten-part shape that Sequoia Capital
famously published as a template for the business-plan / pitch-deck
narrative, explains why the shape works, and shows how to write
the same underlying narrative in four re-frames — one per audience
— without losing coherence.

## The ten parts, in order

The Sequoia-shape narrative has ten parts. Each part answers one
specific question. The order is not arbitrary — each part sets up
the ones that follow.

1. **Company purpose.** One sentence: what the company does, in
   language a reasonably informed non-expert can understand.
2. **Problem.** What is broken today, for whom, at what cost. Not
   the world's problem — the specific pain of a specific customer
   type.
3. **Solution.** What you do about the problem, in one paragraph.
   The mechanism, not the outcome.
4. **Why now.** What changed in the world — technology, regulation,
   customer behaviour, cost curve, distribution — that makes the
   solution possible now, when it wasn't feasible or was too
   expensive before.
5. **Market size.** Who buys, how many of them there are, how much
   they can pay. Top-down and bottom-up if both are available;
   bottom-up if only one.
6. **Competition.** Who else solves this or something adjacent to
   it — including the do-nothing default and internal
   alternatives — and how you win in the segments you're going
   after.
7. **Product.** What the product actually is, ideally with
   evidence it works (a screenshot, a video, a data table, a
   customer quote).
8. **Business model.** How you make money. Who pays, how much, on
   what cadence, what the economics look like.
9. **Team.** Who is doing this, why the specific people are the
   right ones, and what's missing that you plan to hire.
10. **Financials.** What the current numbers are, what the plan
    projects, and how the projection connects to the KRs and the
    market-size argument.

Ten parts. In the deck form, one slide per part. In the memo form,
one paragraph per part. In the two-minute-verbal form, one sentence
per part.

The shape is the shape. What changes across the audience re-frames
(below) is not the *order* or the *number*; it is the *depth* and
the *emphasis* of each part.

## Why this shape

Two things make this specific ten-part shape work, and both are
worth naming so you don't rearrange the parts without reason.

**Reason 1 — each part sets up the ones that follow.** Problem
without purpose is a rant; solution without problem is a feature
tour; why-now without solution is a trend deck; market-size without
why-now is an assertion; competition without market is a namedrop
list; product without competition is a demo; business model without
product is a pricing page; team without business model is an about
page; financials without any of the above are numbers detached from
a narrative. The ten-part order makes each part **earn** its
predecessor before it lands.

**Reason 2 — every audience wants a different subset, in the same
order.** A customer wants parts 1, 2, 3, 7 heavily and skims the
rest. An early employee wants parts 1, 3, 4, 9 heavily and skims
the rest. An investor wants parts 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 heavily and
weights the rest. But every audience wants the same story
underneath — the same purpose, the same problem framing, the same
why-now. The shape is what makes the same underlying story
re-framable four times without contradicting itself.

## The four audience re-frames

The narrative is not written four times; it is written once and
re-framed four times for the four audiences. Each re-frame keeps
all ten parts but re-weights depth and specificity.

### Re-frame 1 — for the co-founder

**Audience.** The person considering joining you as an equal, or
the person you are already working with as an equal. Their decision
is *do I bet my career on this?*.

**What they need to see.**
- **Purpose (1).** In enough depth to know whether you agree on
  what the company is about at the level that matters over a
  decade.
- **Problem (2) and solution (3).** In enough depth to know
  whether you agree on *what the company will actually be doing*
  in year one.
- **Why now (4).** In enough depth to know whether the founder is
  chasing a trend or has a load-bearing thesis.
- **Team (9).** Especially the *what's missing* part — the
  co-founder is going to be part of the answer to what's missing,
  and they need to see that gap named honestly.
- **Financials (10).** Enough to know current cash, current burn,
  and the runway inside which the two-of-you have to prove the
  bet. This is founder-numbers depth (mod-005).

**What is skimmed.** Market size (5), competition (6), and
business model (8) at surface level — the co-founder needs to know
they exist and are defensible, not to underwrite them.

**What is unique to this re-frame.** *Radical honesty about the
gap between what is true today and what the narrative claims will
be true.* A co-founder deceived at the recruiting stage will find
out inside three months; the deception costs the company the
co-founder and the founder's credibility.

### Re-frame 2 — for the first employee

**Audience.** The person considering leaving a job to join a
pre-seed / seed startup. Their decision is *is this a career
step, not a career risk?*.

**What they need to see.**
- **Purpose (1).** One sentence, memorable, so they can explain
  the company to their spouse, their friends, their parents.
- **Solution (3) and product (7).** In enough depth to know what
  they'd be building or selling every day.
- **Why now (4).** So they trust the timing is not fashion.
- **Team (9).** Especially who is already here, so they can
  imagine themselves in the room with those specific people.
- **Financials (10).** Runway in months — how long they have to
  prove themselves before the next round has to happen.

**What is skimmed.** Market size, competition, and business model
at high level.

**What is unique to this re-frame.** *A concrete picture of what
their day-one to day-ninety looks like* — what they would ship in
their first month, what they would own by month three, what
success in the role looks like from the founder's perspective.
This is not in the ten-part shape directly; it is the specific
narrative bridge from "here is the company" to "here is your seat."

### Re-frame 3 — for the first customer

**Audience.** A prospect at the top of the sales funnel or in
early evaluation. Their decision is *is this a real thing I should
spend my time evaluating and eventually my money on?*.

**What they need to see.**
- **Problem (2).** Explained back to them in their own language,
  with their specific version of the pain. This is the most
  load-bearing part of the customer re-frame; if the founder
  cannot state the customer's problem better than the customer
  can, the customer discounts everything downstream.
- **Solution (3) and product (7).** How you solve it and what the
  product looks like when they use it.
- **Business model (8).** How much it costs and how they buy.
- **Team (9).** Only enough to establish credibility — the
  customer is not deciding on your team; they are deciding on
  whether you'll still be here in two years.
- **Financials (10).** Only if asked. Some customers ask
  (procurement, enterprise, regulated industries); most don't.

**What is skimmed.** Company purpose (kept short — it lives in
one line), why now, market size, competition (a customer
generally does not want to hear about your competitors).

**What is unique to this re-frame.** *Evidence that the product
does what it claims* — a working demo, a case study, a data
table, a set of customer references. The customer re-frame is
where the narrative most closely touches reality, because the
customer has the most immediate means to check.

### Re-frame 4 — for the first investor

**Audience.** A seed-stage investor, an angel, or a fund partner
evaluating whether the company is worth their capital and their
board time. Their decision is *do I underwrite this founder to
turn the current early evidence into a scalable business?*.

**What they need to see.**
- **Why now (4).** In depth, because their whole thesis is that
  timing plus mechanism plus team plus market equals underwritten
  risk.
- **Market size (5).** In depth, both top-down and bottom-up.
- **Competition (6).** In depth, including the do-nothing
  competitor and internal-build alternatives at each target
  customer.
- **Business model (8).** In depth — pricing, margins, projected
  unit economics vocabulary from mod-005.
- **Financials (10).** In depth — cash, burn, runway, growth,
  and forward plan against the money they would put in.

**What is skimmed.** Not skimmed exactly — the other five parts
(purpose, problem, solution, product, team) are treated with
depth too, but the emphasis lands on the five listed above
because those are the parts a typical seed investor is
under-informed on and the founder has more information about.

**What is unique to this re-frame.** *A version of the financials
that connects the KRs (chapter 04) to the market-size argument*.
The chain "here is the market, here is the growth we've earned in
it so far, here is the KR set for the next quarter, here is what
that adds up to over the runway the round buys" is what an
investor is underwriting.

Depth on the full investor deck — the actual slide-by-slide
structure of the seed and Series-A decks, the sensitivity
scenarios, the term-sheet conversation — lives in
[`startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum`](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum).
This chapter's job is to say: *the investor deck is a re-frame of
the same ten-part shape, not a different story*.

## Writing the narrative — practical process

The narrative is *not* written by opening a deck template and
filling in bullet points slide by slide. Two better approaches.

**Approach 1 — the memo first.** Write the ten parts as one memo,
one paragraph per part, in prose. Aim for 800–1500 words total.
This is the working artifact from which all four audience
re-frames and the eventual deck are derived. The memo lives in
your operating notes; the deck lives in a slides tool.

Why memo first: writing paragraphs forces you to notice when a
part is missing content. Bullets forgive gaps; sentences don't.
The single most common cause of a bad pitch deck is a founder who
went to bullets before they could write the paragraphs.

**Approach 2 — the two-minute verbal first.** Write the ten parts
as one sentence each — a 300-word script you can deliver in two
minutes. Read it out loud to a friend, a mentor, or a recorder.
Where you stumble is where the underlying story is unclear.

Both approaches have the same property: they *start* with the
underlying content and *derive* the presentation from it. Starting
from the presentation (opening a deck) produces the well-known
failure mode of a beautiful deck with no argument.

## Common failure modes

**1. Purpose sentence that describes the product, not the
company.** "We're building a Slack integration for accounting
firms." That is what you sell, not what you're for. Purpose is at
one level of abstraction above the product; the product is the
current mechanism for the purpose. If you write a purpose
sentence and it wouldn't be true if you pivoted the product, the
purpose is at the wrong level.

**2. Problem that is the founder's problem, not the customer's.**
"Small businesses have too many tools" is a founder's
observation. "Our accounting-firm ICP loses an average of six
hours a week reconciling client-facing spreadsheets with their
practice-management software" is a customer's problem. The
difference is measured by whether the customer would recognise
their own life in the sentence.

**3. Why-now that is just a trend list.** "AI is changing
everything." "Remote work is here to stay." These are not
why-nows; they are backdrops. A real why-now is a specific
enabling change (a new API, a new regulation, a new cost curve, a
new customer expectation) that made the specific solution
feasible or affordable in a way it wasn't 24 months ago.

**4. Market size that is top-down only.** "The global widgets
market is $50B." True and useless. A market-size argument that
does not connect the top-down number to a bottom-up "here are the
segments, here is how many customers each has, here is what they
pay" is not defensible. Investors know the difference; so do good
customers.

**5. Competition slide that lists nobody.** "There are no direct
competitors." Wrong, and specifically wrong in a way that tells
the reader you haven't done the work. The do-nothing option is a
competitor; internal builds are competitors; adjacent tools that
users hack into the shape are competitors. If you truly have no
direct competitors, that is a problem to explain, not a strength
to claim.

**6. Team slide that is a resume dump.** The team section is not
biographies. It is *why the specific people at this cap table are
the right ones to solve this specific problem*. That connection —
person to problem — is what makes the team section land. Bios go
in an appendix.

## When the narrative is re-authored

The narrative is not a weekly artifact. It changes only at major
inflections:

- A pivot in the problem or the solution (parts 2 or 3).
- A material change in the market thesis (parts 4 or 5).
- A material change in the business model (part 8).
- A material change in the team — a co-founder in or out, a key
  hire made, a co-founder role change (part 9).
- Preparation for a fundraise (all parts, re-check for the
  investor re-frame).

Expect the narrative to be re-authored roughly once every 6-12
months at PRE-SEED / SEED. If you are re-writing it every month,
you have a problem below the narrative — the company itself is
changing shape too fast for a decision-log-driven pivot cadence
to be tracking. If you are not re-writing it at all in a year,
the narrative is probably lagging the company; run through the
inflection-triggers above.

## Summary

- The founder-narrative has ten parts in a specific order — company
  purpose, problem, solution, why now, market size, competition,
  product, business model, team, financials — as widely popularised
  in the Sequoia Capital business-plan template.
- The order is load-bearing: each part earns its predecessor before
  it lands.
- The narrative is written once and re-framed four times — for the
  co-founder, the first employee, the first customer, and the first
  investor — with the same underlying ten parts and re-weighted
  depth per audience.
- Write the memo first (one paragraph per part) or the two-minute
  verbal first (one sentence per part). Do *not* start from the
  deck template.
- Sales narrative depth belongs in
  `startup-product-gtm-curriculum`; investor-deck depth belongs in
  `startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum`; this module owns the
  ten-part shape and the four re-frames.
