# The First Investor Update — One Page, Three Sections

## Motivation

Chapter 05 covered the *founder-narrative* — the once-per-inflection
communication that carries the whole story. This chapter covers the
much shorter, much more frequent artifact: the **monthly investor
update**. One page, three sections — **highlights, lowlights, asks**
— sent on a monthly cadence, to a list that at PRE-SEED often has
zero outside investors on it and is written anyway.

The single most important claim in this chapter is that last one:
**write the investor update before there are investors reading it.**
The habit, the shape, and the discipline are worth building at the
zero-audience stage, because by the time an audience arrives — a
first check, a first fund partner, a first mentor who says "keep me
posted" — the shape is already muscle memory. Founders who first
attempt an investor update in the middle of their first fundraise
usually miss the point of the exercise; the update is a *founder*
artifact that happens to be read by investors, not an investor
artifact the founder has to compose from scratch.

## The one-page, three-section shape

The whole update fits on one page. It has exactly three sections,
in a specific order.

### Section 1 — highlights

**What it is.** The three-to-five things the founder is most proud
of, or that most moved the company forward, since the last update.

**Discipline.** Each highlight is one line. Each line names a
specific, checkable event or number — not an adjective. "Signed
Acme as our first six-figure customer" is a highlight; "great month
for sales" is not. If a highlight cannot be reduced to one line
with one specific event or number, it either isn't a highlight or
needs to be split.

**What good looks like.** Three to five items. Any of:
- A specific customer close or expansion, named.
- A metric that moved past a target on the current KRs.
- A hire, an investor, or an advisor that came on.
- A product ship that a customer specifically responded to.
- A milestone that unblocks a downstream workstream.

### Section 2 — lowlights

**What it is.** The two-to-four things that didn't go the way they
were supposed to, and the founder's current thinking on each.

**Discipline.** Each lowlight is one-to-two lines: what happened,
and one line of *what you are doing about it or thinking about it*.
Not "sales was hard this month" — "sales cycle length for our top
segment stretched from 42 to 68 days; we're testing a scaled-down
pilot offer to shorten the eval before we commit more founder-time
to it."

**Why lowlights are load-bearing.** This is the section that founders
most want to omit at the zero-audience stage and most want to soften
at the outside-investor stage. It is the section that carries most
of the artifact's value. Two reasons:

1. **For the founder themselves.** Writing lowlights forces the
   founder to name what is going wrong in language other people
   could read, which is the same forcing function that catches
   self-deception. Nobody can lie to themselves in writing for very
   long; the shape of the sentence gives away the wobble in the
   thinking.
2. **For any investor.** An update with no lowlights is not a
   report; it is a marketing document. Every experienced investor
   discounts the highlights of a highlights-only update because
   they can no longer distinguish signal from PR. A founder who
   writes honest lowlights builds the credibility the highlights
   need to be believed.

Founders who skip section 2 on the first update almost always
have to be told to add it. Once added, they discover the section
is the one they now write first, because it is the one the rest of
the update turns on.

### Section 3 — asks

**What it is.** The two-to-four specific things the founder is
asking the reader for.

**Discipline.** Each ask is one line and names *a specific action a
reader could take*. Not "any thoughts welcome"; that is a wave, not
an ask. Better: "intros to VP-Eng at insurance-tech seed / series-A
companies who might advise on the SOC 2 process," or "any warm
intros to Series-A investors thinking about vertical-SaaS in
regulated industries — targeting a $6-9M round in Q3." Asks that
name the specific action and the specific criterion produce
responses; vague asks produce silence.

**Why asks work.** The most-repeated founder observation about
investor updates is that *the asks section is where updates
generate more value than they consume*. Readers cannot help unless
they know what would help. Named asks convert a nice-to-read note
into a request-for-action; the reader either has the specific
thing (a hire, an intro, an answer, a customer) or knows someone
who does. Nobody offers help they weren't asked for.

**At zero-audience stage.** Even with no outside readers, write
the asks. The asks become the founder's own to-do list for the
month — the specific gaps the update surfaced. When the audience
arrives, the asks section is already sharp.

## Length ceiling

**One page.** Full stop. If it doesn't fit, cut highlights first
(cut to three), then lowlights (cut to two), then asks (cut to
two). The one-page constraint is the artifact's shape; violating
it makes the update fail the very purpose it exists for.

Do not include:
- Screenshots. If a customer sent something worth including, a
  one-line paraphrase with attribution is stronger than an image.
- Charts. If a number matters enough for a chart, it belongs in
  section 1 or 2 as a one-line update; the chart itself belongs in
  an appendix or on the metrics one-pager.
- Explanations of concepts (what a SAFE is, what OKRs are, what
  the ICP is). The audience either knows or is not the audience for
  the update.
- Anecdotes without action. If a customer conversation was
  memorable but didn't change anything, it does not belong.

## The monthly cadence

The update fires **once a month**, on a fixed day. First Tuesday
of the month, first Monday, last Friday — the specific day does
not matter; the fixed-ness does. The audience learns to expect the
update; the founder learns to write against a deadline; the
cadence itself becomes the smallest reliable rhythm the founder
maintains to an outside audience.

Two cadence rules from experience worth naming:

**Rule 1 — never skip.** The failure mode of monthly updates is
skipping a month "because there's nothing to say." Nothing-to-say
months are the ones that most need the update; they are the
months where a founder's own week-to-week busyness has drowned
out the fact that the company did not move. Write the update
anyway — with a shorter highlights section, with a longer
lowlights section, and with an honest ask if there is one. A
short update is fine; a skipped month is not.

**Rule 2 — never batch.** The failure mode of skipping is
followed by the failure mode of *batching two months into one*.
Batching produces an unreadable update that hides which month
things happened in and papers over the skipped-month problem. If
you missed last month, send the current month's update on the
current month's cadence and move on.

## Why write before there are investors

The claim from the top of the chapter — write the update *before*
you have investors reading it — deserves specific defence, because
it looks like process theatre and isn't.

**Reason 1 — the shape is the value.** The three-section shape
forces a founder to distinguish between what moved (highlights),
what didn't (lowlights), and what would unstick things (asks) in a
way weekly cadence artifacts don't. Weekly artifacts optimise for
tactical continuity; the monthly update optimises for *stepping
back*. That step-back is worth doing regardless of audience.

**Reason 2 — the discipline is not portable.** A founder who first
writes their first update during a fundraise is trying to (a)
learn the shape, (b) be honest about lowlights while trying to
close a round, and (c) actually close the round — all at once. The
shape and the honesty are the parts that get compromised. A
founder who has been writing to zero outside investors for four
months is already writing the same update at fundraise time, just
with more readers.

**Reason 3 — the update writes itself later.** Writing an update
against four weeks of Friday shipped-and-learneds and a current
metrics one-pager (chapter 02) takes under an hour. Writing an
update against nothing takes an afternoon. The habit of monthly
updates is what makes future updates cheap.

**Reason 4 — the audience is the founder's own future self.** The
first six-to-twelve monthly updates become an unmatched
retrospective when the company has an inflection — a pivot, a
successful raise, a failed raise, a co-founder split, a
first-employee hire. The founder reading their own updates back is
the check on "we always thought that" (chapter 03's decision log
covers the same failure at higher resolution).

## Who reads it at zero-audience stage

If there are no outside investors, the "list" is still non-empty.
Reasonable readers at the zero-audience stage:
- **A co-founder** — even one you talk to every day; the written
  update is different from conversation.
- **A first employee**, once there is one.
- **Two-to-three mentors or advisors** — the people who have
  earned the right to see the whole picture.
- **The founder's own operating notes**, as the last file
  archived to the month's folder.

At the zero-audience stage, the reader that matters most is the
last one — future you. Six months of your own monthly updates is
one of the most useful decision-support artifacts a founder can
build.

## How the update interlocks with the loop

The update is the monthly artifact that summarises the loop's
weekly outputs. It should be *authored from* the loop's artifacts,
not from scratch:

- **Highlights** come from the last four Friday shipped-and-learned
  "Shipped" sections. Scan them, pick the three-to-five that a
  reader would care about, one line each.
- **Lowlights** come from the last four "Learned" sections and any
  KR that has been off-track for two-plus consecutive weeks
  (chapter 04). If none of those exist, the update is missing
  section 2 — either the month really was frictionless (rare) or
  the shipped-and-learneds are being written dishonestly (usually
  the case).
- **Asks** come from the "what next week has to carry" sections
  of the last four shipped-and-learneds, filtered for items that
  outside help could accelerate. The Monday plan for the coming
  month is where the asks land after the update.
- **Numbers** in the update — the founder-numbers row, the
  North-Star row, the KR row — are the same numbers on the
  current metrics one-pager. The update quotes the one-pager; it
  does not re-derive them.

Concretely, the update takes about 45–60 minutes to write when
the weekly cadence has been running. When it doesn't have that
substrate, the update takes an afternoon and is usually worse.
This is why the update is the *last* artifact in the module to be
introduced but the *first* to be built on top of the others.

## Common failure modes

**1. Highlights-only updates.** Ship two-to-three of these and the
audience — even the audience of one, the founder themselves —
stops trusting the update. Once trust is lost, the update is
performing worse than no update at all. Fix by writing section 2
first, deliberately, for the next three months.

**2. Vague asks.** "Any advice welcome." "Let me know if you can
help." These generate zero responses because they name no action.
Fix by writing the ask in the form *action + criterion + timing*:
"intros to [specific role] at [specific company shape] by [rough
timing]."

**3. Length inflation.** The first update is one page. The
second is one and a half. By month six the update is a
three-page recap that nobody reads. Fix with the one-page hard
ceiling and the cut-order above (highlights first, then
lowlights, then asks). If a longer document is genuinely useful,
it lives in an appendix; the update itself stays one page.

**4. Update as marketing.** Some founders use the update as a
place to test messaging. This is a real use, but it corrupts the
update — the audience notices when highlights are written for
their PR value rather than their signal value. Marketing testing
belongs on the marketing site or in a dedicated audience test;
the update is for signal.

**5. Slack thread as update.** Some founders send the update as
a Slack post, in bullets, without a subject line, in a channel.
This works at zero-audience stage for exactly one month; by
month three the messages are unfindable. The update should be
one file, in one folder, retrievable by month. Email works,
Notion works, a plain-text file works; ephemeral channels do not.

## Summary

- The first investor update is one page, three sections —
  **highlights, lowlights, asks** — sent on a fixed monthly
  cadence.
- Each highlight names one specific event or number. Each lowlight
  names what happened *and* one line on what you're doing about it.
  Each ask names a specific action a reader could take.
- Write the update from month zero, even with no outside
  investors. The shape is the value; the audience arrives later.
- Author the update from the four Friday shipped-and-learneds and
  the current metrics one-pager. The update is a summary of the
  weekly loop, not a fresh composition.
- Never skip. Never batch. Never let it grow past one page.
- Depth on later-stage updates and board packs lives in the
  finance-fundraising and operations-governance pillars.
