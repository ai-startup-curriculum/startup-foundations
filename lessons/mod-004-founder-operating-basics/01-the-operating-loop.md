# The Operating Loop — Decide, Allocate, Communicate, Measure

## Motivation

The three prior modules built the founder a map and a clock. mod-002's
graph told them *where* every business thing lives. mod-003's stage
ladder told them *which nodes matter this week*. Neither one told them
what to actually do at 9 a.m. on Monday.

The dominant failure mode of the well-read founder at PRE-SEED is not
that they don't know what matters. It is that the *shape of the week*
is set by the last message in their inbox rather than by any deliberate
choice they made on Monday. Attention flows to whatever is loudest;
capital flows to whatever felt urgent when the invoice arrived; the
team gets communication in the form of Slack pings and post-hoc
context; the numbers get looked at when someone asks. The founder
looks up at Friday afternoon and cannot name the three most important
decisions of the week or why they were made.

The fix is not more discipline. Discipline is a resource the founder
already spends. The fix is a **loop** — a small, repeatable, weekly
shape that constrains where attention, capital, communication, and
measurement go, so the founder is not re-inventing the shape of the
week every seven days.

This chapter installs that loop. The remaining six chapters of the
module each formalise one component of it (chapters 02–06) or draw the
ownership boundary around it (chapter 07).

## The four-part loop

The loop this module teaches has four parts:

1. **Decide.** Make the calls that only the founder can make, and make
   them on a cadence, not on an event trigger.
2. **Allocate.** Move the three scarce founder resources — attention,
   capital, and hires — to where the decisions from step 1 said they
   should go.
3. **Communicate.** Make the decisions and the allocations legible to
   the four audiences that need them — the co-founder, the team,
   customers, and investors.
4. **Measure.** Instrument the outcome so next week's decisions can be
   made on evidence rather than vibes.

The loop runs once a week. Monday is the plan (decide + allocate).
Wednesday is a mid-week check on the metrics. Friday is the review
(measure + communicate). The week is the atomic unit because it is
short enough that reality catches drift fast, and long enough that
real work fits between the endpoints.

The rest of this chapter walks each of the four parts, names the
failure mode it defends against, and points at the chapter that
formalises the corresponding artifact.

## Decide

**What the founder does.** Make the calls that only they can make —
the ones that require founder-level context, founder-level authority,
or founder-level accountability — and make them at deliberate points
in the week rather than whenever the question happens to land.

**What that looks like at PRE-SEED.** A pre-seed founder has roughly
five to fifteen founder-only calls a week: pick this week's biggest
customer bet, hire vs. contract for the next role, kill vs. push a
half-working feature, take vs. refuse a partnership offer, spend vs.
save the discretionary budget line, pivot vs. persevere on a stalled
motion. Each of these is a specific, dated call with an owner (the
founder) and a reason.

**The failure mode.** The failure mode this part defends against is
**decision-by-drift** — the pattern where the founder never makes a
call at all, and the situation resolves itself six weeks later in the
direction of least resistance. Drift produces the same outcomes an
explicit call would have produced, minus the ability to learn from
them (because no one wrote down the reason) and minus the ability to
change course early (because no one noticed the call was made).

**The artifact this part produces.** The **decision log** — one row
per call, dated, one line of reason. Chapter 03 formalises the log
and the entry shape. The log is the memory the "decide" step writes
to.

## Allocate

**What the founder does.** Move the three scarce founder resources
— attention, capital, hires — to where the decisions from the
"decide" step said they should go, in an amount proportional to the
size of the call.

**What that looks like at PRE-SEED.** *Attention* allocation is the
Monday plan: this week the founder will spend X hours on this
customer, Y hours on this ship, Z hours on this hire. *Capital*
allocation is the small handful of spend calls — buy the tool, delay
the hire, top up the ad budget. *Hires* allocation, at PRE-SEED, is
almost always "not yet" — but the timing of the "yet" is a founder
call and it belongs in the log.

**The failure mode.** The failure mode this part defends against is
**attention arbitrage** — the pattern where the founder's attention
gets pulled by whichever stakeholder is loudest that week (a
demanding early customer, a chatty prospective investor, a
half-broken vendor). The Monday plan is the pre-commitment that
makes the arbitrage visible: when the day looks nothing like Monday
said it would, the founder can see the drift and choose to accept
it, resist it, or update the plan on purpose.

**The artifact this part produces.** The **Monday plan** — three to
five things this week, each with a hours-of-founder-attention budget
next to it. Chapter 02 formalises the plan and its pair, the Friday
shipped-and-learned.

## Communicate

**What the founder does.** Make the decisions and the allocations
legible to the four audiences that need them — the co-founder or
first employee, the team, current or candidate customers, and
current or candidate investors — in the *shape those audiences can
act on*.

**What that looks like at PRE-SEED.** The four communication
artifacts, in order of frequency:

- **To the co-founder / first employee**: the Monday plan and the
  Friday shipped-and-learned. Two artifacts a week, both structured,
  both take under 15 minutes to write.
- **To the team** (when there is one — often the same as the
  co-founder / first employee at PRE-SEED): the same two artifacts,
  plus any decisions from the log that affect them.
- **To customers**: the founder-narrative in the Sequoia shape,
  compressed and reframed for the customer conversation. The
  narrative is written once (chapter 05) and re-used for every
  customer conversation the founder has this quarter.
- **To investors** (whether or not there are outside investors yet):
  the **first investor update** — one page, three sections
  (highlights, lowlights, asks), monthly cadence. Chapter 06
  formalises it.

**The failure mode.** The failure mode this part defends against is
**private context** — the pattern where the reason for every call
lives only in the founder's head. Private context is fine for one
week; over a quarter it compounds into a team that cannot make
independent decisions because they cannot reconstruct why the last
five decisions were made. The four communication artifacts are the
scaffolding that lets other people carry founder-level context.

**The artifacts this part produces.** The Sequoia-shape narrative
(chapter 05), the first investor update (chapter 06), and the
communication reuse of the Monday plan and Friday
shipped-and-learned (chapter 02).

## Measure

**What the founder does.** Instrument the outcome of the week so
next week's decisions can be made on evidence rather than vibes,
and instrument the goals of the quarter so the weeks compound
toward something.

**What that looks like at PRE-SEED.** Two layers of measurement:

- **Weekly** — a **metrics one-pager** with a small number of numbers
  the founder has decided in advance are the ones that matter this
  quarter. mod-005 (`Reading the Numbers`) is where the founder-numbers
  themselves — runway, burn, growth rate, default alive / default
  dead, chosen North-Star — are authored; this module's chapter 02
  formalises the *cadence* around them.
- **Quarterly** — a **lightweight OKR shape** sized for two or three
  people: one company objective, three key results, weekly check-in
  on the KRs. Chapter 04 formalises the shape and, more importantly,
  names the four anti-patterns that make OKRs go sideways at this
  scale.

**The failure mode.** The failure mode this part defends against is
**vibes-based reasoning** — the pattern where the founder feels the
week went well or badly and then reasons backward to justify the
feeling. Vibes-based reasoning is not usually wrong at the
short-time-horizon it operates on; it is wrong when it has to
support decisions that compound over months. The weekly numbers and
the quarterly OKR are the two checks that catch compounding vibes
before they turn into a wasted quarter.

**The artifacts this part produces.** The metrics one-pager (chapter
02) and the OKR shape (chapter 04).

## Why weekly

The loop runs at a weekly cadence, not a daily one and not a monthly
one. Three reasons.

**Daily is too short.** Real founder work — a customer conversation
that becomes a spec that becomes a shipped change — has a natural
cycle time longer than a day. A daily version of the loop would
reduce to a stand-up, and stand-ups at two or three people
degenerate into status theatre inside a month.

**Monthly is too long.** Founder work at PRE-SEED and SEED has to
adapt to reality faster than a month. A month between check-ins is
a month a founder can spend chasing the wrong customer, holding the
wrong hire open, or letting a small metric problem compound into a
big one. Investor updates run monthly (chapter 06) because the
audience needs the summary, not because the founder's own loop
should.

**Weekly matches the artifact cycle.** The Monday plan, Friday
shipped-and-learned, metrics one-pager, and decision-log review all
sit inside one week naturally. The OKR check-in is weekly too; the
narrative and the investor update are lower-frequency and are
authored *outside* the weekly loop but *fed by* it.

The founder's calendar over a normal week ends up looking roughly
like:

- **Monday morning (30–60 min)** — Monday plan; review last week's
  decision-log entries; skim the metrics one-pager for anything
  broken.
- **Wednesday mid-morning (10–15 min)** — metrics check; if a
  number moved, name why and update the plan if needed.
- **Friday afternoon (45–60 min)** — Friday shipped-and-learned;
  update the metrics one-pager with the week's close; log any
  decisions made this week that are not already logged; check the
  OKR key-result progress against plan.

That is roughly two hours a week of "loop overhead" — the cost of
making the week deliberate. Every founder who has ever run without
the loop describes the cost of *not* running it as considerably
higher.

## The five artifacts that carry the loop

The loop is not held in the founder's head; it is held in five
cheap, reusable artifacts. Each is small, each takes minutes to
update, and each is the input to at least one other artifact in the
set:

| Artifact | Cadence | Chapter | The failure mode it defends against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monday plan** | weekly | 02 | Attention arbitrage — the loudest stakeholder wins |
| **Friday shipped-and-learned** | weekly | 02 | Amnesia — the week is not remembered a month later |
| **Metrics one-pager** | weekly | 02 | Vibes reasoning — feelings replace numbers |
| **Decision log** | continuous | 03 | Revisionism — "we always thought that" |
| **First investor update** | monthly | 06 | Private context — the reasons live only in the founder's head |

Plus two artifacts authored on a slower cadence (quarterly and one
per major inflection):

| Artifact | Cadence | Chapter | The failure mode it defends against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OKR (one objective, three KRs)** | quarterly + weekly check-in | 04 | Weeks that don't compound into a quarter |
| **Sequoia-shape narrative** | one per major inflection | 05 | Four audiences that can't act on the same story |

**Seven artifacts, five of them weekly-or-continuous, all of them
one page or less.** The whole shape is deliberately small. The
temptation at PRE-SEED is to skip the shape entirely because it
feels bureaucratic; the temptation at SEED is to over-build it into
an operating manual. Both fail. The seven artifacts above are the
minimum viable operating shape — anything less and the failure
modes named above start eating the week; anything more and the
process itself becomes a job.

## How this module builds on the loop

The rest of the module is a chapter per component or artifact, plus
the ownership boundary.

- **Chapter 02** formalises the three weekly cadence artifacts —
  Monday plan, Friday shipped-and-learned, metrics one-pager — and
  the interlocks between them.
- **Chapter 03** formalises the decision log.
- **Chapter 04** formalises the OKR shape at 2-3 people and names
  the four anti-patterns.
- **Chapter 05** formalises the Sequoia-shape founder narrative and
  the four-audience re-frame.
- **Chapter 06** formalises the first investor update and the case
  for writing it before there are outside investors.
- **Chapter 07** draws the boundary — this module owns the loop;
  operations depth, product/GTM operating loops, and capital
  allocation live in the pillar curricula.

The order matters. The decision log (chapter 03) writes down the
output of "decide"; the Monday plan and metrics one-pager (chapter
02) are the output of "allocate" and "measure"; the OKR (chapter 04)
sits between "decide" and "measure" as the quarterly commitment the
weekly measurements are checked against; the narrative (chapter 05)
and investor update (chapter 06) are the two shapes of "communicate."
Every artifact is one component of the four-part loop from this
chapter.

## Summary

- The founder's operating loop is four parts on a weekly cadence:
  **decide, allocate, communicate, measure**.
- Each part defends against a specific failure mode: decision-by-drift,
  attention arbitrage, private context, and vibes reasoning
  respectively.
- The loop is carried by seven artifacts — five weekly / continuous
  (Monday plan, Friday shipped-and-learned, metrics one-pager,
  decision log, investor update) and two on slower cadences (OKR,
  Sequoia-shape narrative).
- Weekly is the right cadence: daily degenerates to stand-up
  theatre, monthly lets drift compound.
- The whole loop costs roughly two hours a week of overhead — the
  price of a deliberate week.
