# Lean Canvas vs Business Model Canvas

## Motivation

The graph tells you *where* a founder's questions live. A **canvas** tells
you *what the whole set of assumptions under your startup looks like on a
single page*. The two working canvases in the field — the **Business Model
Canvas** (Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, *Business Model Generation*,
2010) and the **Lean Canvas** (Ash Maurya, *Running Lean*, 2010; a Lean
Startup–oriented adaptation of Osterwalder's canvas) — are the two most
widely used one-page shapes for "this is the assumption set my company is
built on."

Foundations does not teach either canvas to depth — depth lives in the
canonical books and in the pillar curricula. It teaches you that both
exist, that they are two *tools for two jobs*, and that using the right one
matters. This chapter walks the two canvases side by side and gives you a
rule for choosing between them.

## Where the canvases fit on the graph

A canvas is a *cross-branch* artifact — it touches nodes across the
customer-facing, corporate-structure, and economics branches all at once.
That is by design: the whole point is a one-page view of the entire
business as an assumption set.

On the graph, both canvases primarily live *upstream of everything else*,
attached to Foundations itself. They are the artifacts you would write
before any specific downstream node has been worked in earnest, and the
artifacts you would revise every time evidence changes the assumption set.
Foundations owns them as *positions*; depth is in the canonical books and
in the appropriate pillar curriculum for whichever cell you are
sharpening.

## The Business Model Canvas — nine building blocks

The **Business Model Canvas** (BMC), published by Osterwalder and Pigneur
in *Business Model Generation* (2010, Wiley), is a one-page nine-block
template describing how any organization creates, delivers, and captures
value. The nine blocks are:

<!-- needs-research: verify the exact naming of the nine blocks and the current publisher-endorsed layout at strategyzer.com/canvas/business-model-canvas; the wording below reflects the widely-used shorthand and should be reconciled with the primary source before publication. -->

1. **Customer Segments** — for whom are you creating value?
2. **Value Propositions** — what value do you deliver, for which segment?
3. **Channels** — how do the value propositions reach the segments?
4. **Customer Relationships** — what kind of relationship does each
   segment expect (self-serve, high-touch, community, automated)?
5. **Revenue Streams** — for what value are customers willing to pay?
6. **Key Resources** — what assets does the value proposition require
   (people, IP, infrastructure, capital)?
7. **Key Activities** — what actions must the business perform to deliver?
8. **Key Partnerships** — which external parties are essential (suppliers,
   platforms, channel partners)?
9. **Cost Structure** — what are the largest costs implied by the model?

The BMC is designed to describe *any* organization — startups, mature
companies, non-profits, government programs. It is deliberately neutral on
the question of *whether* the model has been validated; it is a
description tool, not an experiment-design tool.

## The Lean Canvas — Maurya's adaptation

The **Lean Canvas**, published by Ash Maurya in *Running Lean* (O'Reilly,
2010; multiple later editions), is a nine-block adaptation of the BMC
that replaces four of Osterwalder's blocks with blocks more directly
useful to an *early-stage founder searching for a business model under
uncertainty*.

The Lean Canvas blocks, with the changes from the BMC called out:

<!-- needs-research: verify the exact naming of the Lean Canvas blocks and confirm which four blocks Ash Maurya replaced from the BMC (canonically: Key Activities, Key Resources, Key Partnerships, Customer Relationships → Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage). Sourcing at leanstack.com. -->

1. **Problem** — the top 1–3 problems you are trying to solve (BMC does
   not have a Problem block; this is the largest single difference).
2. **Customer Segments** — same idea as BMC; Maurya emphasizes naming
   *early adopters* explicitly.
3. **Unique Value Proposition** — a single clear message stating why
   the segment should care.
4. **Solution** — the top 1–3 features of the product that address the
   problems (BMC has no Solution block; this is the second-largest
   difference).
5. **Channels** — same as BMC.
6. **Revenue Streams** — same as BMC.
7. **Cost Structure** — same as BMC.
8. **Key Metrics** — the handful of numbers that tell you if the model
   is working (BMC has no explicit Key Metrics block).
9. **Unfair Advantage** — what the business has that competitors
   cannot easily copy (BMC has no explicit Unfair Advantage block).

The Lean Canvas is deliberately built around **assumptions to be tested**
— Problem, Solution, and Key Metrics are all direct references to the
Lean Startup build-measure-learn loop (mod-001 chapter 04). It is an
experiment-design tool.

## Side-by-side — the four block differences

| BMC block (replaced) | Lean Canvas block (in its place) | Why the swap |
|---|---|---|
| Key Activities | **Problem** | Early-stage: naming the problem to test is more useful than describing operational activities |
| Key Resources | **Solution** | Early-stage: naming the solution hypothesis is more useful than listing required resources |
| Key Partnerships | **Key Metrics** | Early-stage: knowing what you'll measure to know if it works matters more than named partners |
| Customer Relationships | **Unfair Advantage** | Early-stage: honestly answering "why us, why now" matters more than describing customer-relationship type |

The remaining five blocks — Customer Segments, Value Proposition,
Channels, Revenue Streams, Cost Structure — are shared. Which is to say
the two canvases agree on the *core commercial shape* of any business
and differ only on which *supporting* questions matter most at which
stage.

## When each canvas is the better instrument

The choice is not arbitrary. Use the following rule:

> **Use the Lean Canvas when the primary job is validating an early-stage
> hypothesis under uncertainty. Use the Business Model Canvas when the
> primary job is describing a known or mostly-known business model to
> yourself, a team, an investor, or a board.**

The rule follows from the block differences. Problem / Solution / Key
Metrics / Unfair Advantage are the four blocks a founder is *actively
searching* on in the pre-PMF life of a startup. Key Activities / Key
Resources / Key Partnerships / Customer Relationships are the four
blocks that matter more when the model is established and the question is
how to *operate* it well.

The stage-mapped guidance:

| Stage | Preferred canvas | Why |
|---|---|---|
| IDEA | Lean Canvas | Everything is a hypothesis; the four Lean-specific blocks are the ones being tested. |
| PRE-SEED | Lean Canvas | Same as IDEA; use it to track which assumptions have been tested and which haven't. |
| SEED | Lean Canvas, migrating | Problem / Solution start to settle; Key Activities begin to matter as the operating shape crystalises. Some teams run both canvases. |
| SERIES-A | Business Model Canvas | Model is largely known; describing operations, partnerships, and resources matters more than re-naming the problem. |
| GROWTH | Business Model Canvas | The operating description is the primary use case. |
| MATURE | Business Model Canvas | Long past the assumption-testing phase. |

Neither canvas is *better* — they are two instruments for two jobs.
Founders who insist on one canvas past its useful stage are showing you
their identity, not their operating discipline.

## What both canvases share — and what neither replaces

Both canvases share three attributes that make them useful:

- **One page.** Both are designed to fit on a single sheet. That
  constraint forces prioritization and makes the artifact reviewable in
  minutes.
- **Cross-branch view.** Both cover customer-facing (Segments, Value
  Proposition, Channels), corporate-structure-adjacent (Partnerships,
  Resources) and economics (Revenue, Cost) in a single frame.
- **Revisable under evidence.** Both are designed to be re-drawn as the
  business learns. Neither is a "get it right once" artifact.

Both canvases also share three things they *do not* replace:

- **The runway model.** A canvas describes the model; it does not
  quantify how long you have. mod-005 owns the runway model.
- **The financial model.** A canvas is not a P&L; it does not schedule
  costs and revenues over time. That is the Financial Modeling node.
- **The Customer Development interviews.** A canvas is a set of
  hypotheses; it does not by itself validate them against real
  customers. That is Customer Discovery.

A canvas is an *organizing* artifact — it is where your assumptions live
so you can see them at once. It is not a *validating* artifact and it is
not an *operating* artifact.

## How to place canvases on the graph

Using the chapter-05 placement move:

- **Primary node:** Foundations itself (both canvases are cross-branch
  organizing artifacts). If you must attach to a downstream node, the
  Lean Canvas attaches best to `Customer Discovery` (its Problem and
  Solution blocks feed directly into interview scoping); the BMC
  attaches best to `Financial Modeling` and `GTM Strategy` (its blocks
  feed model drivers and channel plans).
- **Upstream dependencies:** almost none — both canvases can be written
  on day one and revised as evidence accumulates.
- **Downstream consumers:** the customer interview script (Lean → Customer
  Discovery); the financial model driver set (BMC → Financial Modeling);
  the pitch narrative (both → Fundraising).
- **Owning repo:** both books cited above; some pillar-curriculum
  reinforcement in
  [`startup-product-gtm-curriculum`](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-product-gtm-curriculum)
  and
  [`startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum`](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum).

## Common failure modes

- **Filling the canvas in as fiction.** Both canvases are only useful if
  the blocks are honest. A Problem block that lists a plausible-sounding
  problem no one has been interviewed about is worse than an empty
  Problem block, because it feels like progress.
- **Freezing the canvas.** Both canvases are supposed to be revised.
  Founders who print out their canvas, frame it, and refuse to change
  it under new evidence are misusing the tool.
- **Using the wrong canvas for the stage.** A pre-seed founder listing
  Key Partnerships and Customer Relationships in a BMC is doing
  operating-mode work in what should be assumption-testing mode. A
  Series-B company still fighting over the Problem block in a Lean
  Canvas is admitting the PMF work was never finished.
- **Substituting the canvas for the deeper work.** A completed canvas is
  not a strategy; it is a snapshot of the assumption set. The strategy
  work — the pillar work — happens against the canvas, not in it.

## Summary

- Two canvases: **Business Model Canvas** (Osterwalder / Pigneur, 2010)
  and **Lean Canvas** (Maurya, 2010, a Lean-Startup-oriented adaptation).
- They share five blocks — Customer Segments, Value Proposition,
  Channels, Revenue, Cost — and differ on four: Lean swaps in Problem /
  Solution / Key Metrics / Unfair Advantage in place of Key Activities
  / Key Resources / Key Partnerships / Customer Relationships.
- **Rule:** Lean Canvas when the primary job is validating a hypothesis
  under uncertainty; BMC when the primary job is describing a known or
  mostly-known model. The stage table above maps this to IDEA →
  MATURE.
- Both canvases are one-page, cross-branch, revisable-under-evidence
  organizing artifacts. Neither replaces the runway model, the
  financial model, or the Customer Development interviews.
- Placement: Foundations itself (cross-branch); Lean feeds Customer
  Discovery, BMC feeds GTM and Financial Modeling; both feed the
  pitch narrative.
