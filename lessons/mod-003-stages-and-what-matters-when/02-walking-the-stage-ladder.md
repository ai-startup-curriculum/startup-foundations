# Walking the Stage Ladder

## Motivation

Chapter 01 named the stage axis and its six rungs. This chapter walks each rung
in enough depth that you can stand in front of a founder — yourself, most
likely — and answer three questions about them: *what stage are they in, what
question are they trying to answer, and what work belongs in this week?*

The three questions form a fixed shape used for every stage in this chapter:

1. **Signal you're here.** The observable evidence that says this stage — not
   the previous one, not the next one — is the accurate description of the
   business today.
2. **The founder's core question.** The single question that, when answered
   with real evidence, unlocks the transition to the next stage. If the
   founder cannot state this question in one sentence, they cannot spend the
   week on it.
3. **Typical focus.** The two or three nodes on the mod-002 graph that
   should be actively worked at this stage — plus the biggest attention-drains
   that pretend to be load-bearing but aren't.

The rows are the same ones in [`STARTUP_STAGES.md`](../../STARTUP_STAGES.md).
That document is the one-line canonical reference; this chapter is the reading
key.

## IDEA

### Signal you're here

- No company yet — or an entity that exists only on paper. No product beyond
  a sketch or a click-through prototype. No customers. No revenue.
- The founder is holding a **hunch and a candidate problem**: a felt sense
  that some group of people has a real, unresolved pain, and a suspicion that
  something could be built for them.
- The evidence base is anecdotal — a conversation the founder had, a personal
  frustration, a pattern noticed while working somewhere else. It is not yet
  systematic.
- If you asked "who is the customer?" the honest answer is a category ("small
  eCommerce shops," "senior finance analysts," "hobbyist woodworkers"), not
  a specific person you've talked to five times.

### The founder's core question

> Is this problem real, sharply-felt, and worth solving?

The whole stage is the **problem search** from mod-001 chapter 05. Nothing
else can be productively answered until this is. Any activity that does not
directly contribute evidence to this question is premature.

### Typical focus

Active work:

- **Customer Discovery** on the mod-002 graph — structured conversations
  with the candidate user segment, in the shape Blank teaches: get out of
  the building, learn the problem in the user's own words, test whether the
  problem is painful enough to spend time or money on.
- **First-principles problem framing** — writing down the problem as you now
  understand it, versus how you originally framed it, and updating that
  framing after each batch of conversations. This is the artifact that
  matures fastest at IDEA.
- **Deciding whether to continue** — some IDEA-stage searches close with
  "the problem is not real, or not painful enough, or already solved."
  Ending well is a valid IDEA-stage outcome.

Attention-drains that pretend to be work at IDEA:

- Building a landing page and tuning the copy. (You do not know what to
  say yet.)
- Optimising ad spend. (You do not have a product to advertise.)
- Reading about SAFEs, term sheets, and cap tables. (You have nothing to
  capitalise.)
- Naming and branding the eventual company. (The name is nearly
  irreplaceable and the eventual company will not resemble the current
  hunch.)
- Recruiting a formal advisory board. (You have nothing to advise on yet.)

The rule at IDEA is severe: **your calendar should look like a customer
research calendar.** If it doesn't, you are not really at IDEA — you're
pretending to have progressed past it.

## PRE-SEED

### Signal you're here

- The problem is real enough that you are willing to **incorporate** or have
  just done so. First formal choices are being made — entity type, founder
  agreements, initial equity split, sometimes a first small check (angel,
  friends-and-family, a small accelerator).
- You are **building v0**: a minimum solution the target user could plausibly
  use. Something that runs, even if it is embarrassing.
- The customer segment has narrowed from a category to a **named group of
  specific users** you have talked to, and you can quote them.
- You have some money — often personal, sometimes a small pre-seed round —
  and a specific amount of runway you're spending against.

### The founder's core question

> Can I produce evidence that some solution to this problem could actually
> work?

This is the **solution search** from mod-001 chapter 05, running alongside
a still-open problem search that reopens when v0 users describe the problem
differently than you did. The question is answered when a small number of
real users pick up v0, use it repeatedly, and choose it over their previous
workaround.

### Typical focus

Active work:

- **Continuing customer discovery** — the problem search doesn't close;
  every v0 usage is more evidence for or against the problem framing.
- **Solution scoping and MVP build** — the smallest thing that could be
  used by a user, not the polished thing. Ries's Build-Measure-Learn loop
  from mod-001 chapter 04 is the operating shape.
- **Incorporation and founder agreements** — vesting, IP assignment,
  co-founder equity split, a clean cap table with nothing on it yet. Not
  because you're about to raise a big round, but because the box needs to
  be clean before it holds anything valuable.
- **First runway math** — starting cash, monthly burn, months of runway
  remaining, and the decision of what the runway is being spent to
  discover. Not a full financial model; a two-line reality check.
- **Hand-work with users** — the entire theme of chapter 04 of this
  module. At PRE-SEED, un-scalable hand-work with users is the work.

Attention-drains that pretend to be work at PRE-SEED:

- Studying the mechanics of a Series-A term sheet.
- Building a compensation-committee charter.
- Studying multinational transfer pricing.
- Building a marketing funnel with paid channels.
- Designing an org chart with roles you have no one to fill.
- Chasing prestige investors for a round you're not yet ready to raise.

The rule at PRE-SEED: **most of your week should be with users and inside
the product.** The other 20% is the incorporation-and-runway hygiene above.

## SEED

### Signal you're here

- Real **early users or early revenue**. The v0 is being used repeatedly by
  a small number of users, or a small number of customers are paying (at
  whatever price you set).
- You are **raising or have raised a first priced round** — or the SAFE
  round you raised is closing and you're spending against it. Even if the
  round is still on SAFEs, the amount is meaningful (typically enough for
  12–24 months of runway) and the fundraising conversation now involves
  metrics.
- You have or are about to make your **first hires** — a small handful of
  people who are neither co-founders nor advisors.
- The founder-led sales motion is on: the founder is personally selling to
  every early customer.

### The founder's core question

> Can I find repeatable value — the same value proposition, sold the same
> way, to the next customer and the one after that — and fund the search
> for product-market fit for as long as it takes?

Two searches are running: the **market search** (is this a coherent segment
whose members behave similarly enough for the same product to work across
them?) and the beginning of the **business-model search** (is there a
pricing, packaging, and sales motion that produces workable unit economics?).
Both are from mod-001 chapter 05.

### Typical focus

Active work:

- **Founder-led sales** — the founder is the salesperson. Each early
  customer is a data point about the market and the pricing.
- **Runway management** — you have real money and a real burn rate. The
  runway model from mod-005 becomes a first-class weekly artifact.
- **First hires** — usually the roles that unbottleneck the founder from
  their highest-leverage work. Do not hire to fill the org chart; hire to
  remove founder bottlenecks.
- **Seed fundraising** — either you raised the round already and are
  spending it against milestones, or you are actively raising it and the
  investor conversations are the source of the fastest external feedback
  on whether the story hangs together.
- **Cap table** — from PRE-SEED's clean-box hygiene to SEED's first real
  dilution event, option pool decisions, and (if applicable) SAFE-to-priced
  conversion.
- **First PMF signals** — retention curves, engagement, a preliminary
  "Sean Ellis test" style question. Not yet PMF; the *hunt* for PMF is on.

Attention-drains that pretend to be work at SEED:

- Board committees. (You have three people on your board — no committees
  needed.)
- Deep unit-economics modeling with sensitivity analysis on assumptions
  you're still discovering.
- Enterprise-style RFP responses to buyers who won't close.
- A GTM team, a marketing team, a sales team, before founder-led sales
  is repeatable.
- International expansion. (You do not yet have a repeatable domestic
  motion.)

The rule at SEED: **founder-led sales, weekly runway check, and the
smallest number of hires that unbottleneck the founder from those two.**
Everything else is a distraction dressed as a milestone.

## SERIES-A

### Signal you're here

- **PMF signals a fund can underwrite** — retention, expansion, referral,
  engagement metrics that pass an institutional-investor filter, not just a
  founder's own gut.
- You have raised — or are raising — a priced round meaningfully larger
  than your SEED round, typically from an institutional lead who takes a
  board seat.
- The company is **growing headcount** past the point where the founder
  knows every hire's daily work. Managers appear. First director hires.
- The **GTM motion** is being built as a repeatable system — a sales
  playbook, a real marketing function, a defined ICP, quotas, dashboards.

### The founder's core question

> Can I turn the working thing into a scalable machine — repeatable GTM,
> underwritable unit economics, an organisation that runs without me in
> every meeting?

This is the **business-model search at scale** from mod-001 chapter 05.
The question is about turning what works into a *system* — not proving the
concept, but building the operating machine around it.

### Typical focus

Active work:

- **Unit economics** as an actual operating instrument — CAC, LTV, payback,
  gross margin, cohort retention. The vocabulary mod-005 introduces becomes
  a daily working set.
- **GTM engine build-out** — a sales team with quotas, a marketing team
  with attribution, a defined ICP, a defined sales cycle. Depth lives in
  `startup-product-gtm-curriculum`.
- **Org design** — the first management layer appears. Founder-as-doer
  gives way (slowly, unevenly) to founder-as-hirer-and-coach.
- **Board governance** — a real board with an institutional director.
  Board meetings, board packs, real cadence. Depth lives in
  `startup-operations-governance-curriculum`.
- **Milestone-driven capital deployment** — the Series A is being spent
  against specific milestones (revenue, growth rate, hires, product
  surface). Capital-allocation discipline arrives.

Attention-drains that pretend to be work at SERIES-A:

- Personally doing PRE-SEED-style customer interviews for every product
  decision. (Some of that work still happens — see mod-001 chapter 05 on
  searches reopening — but if the founder is doing it *instead* of building
  the machine, they've regressed.)
- International expansion before the domestic motion is fully scaled.
- Second and third product lines while the first one still needs
  scaling.
- IPO planning. (You are years away.)
- M&A of small competitors as a scaling strategy. (You need to scale the
  motion first, not buy revenue.)

The rule at SERIES-A: **you are building the machine, not selling the
first widgets by hand.** If you are still the salesperson on every deal,
you are still SEED with a bigger cap table.

## GROWTH

### Signal you're here

- Repeatable, defensible growth. The machine works — new customers arrive
  at a predictable cost, retain at a predictable rate, expand at a
  predictable rate.
- **Scaling headcount and spend meaningfully** — hundreds of employees, a
  functional org (product, engineering, sales, marketing, finance, people,
  legal), multiple offices or full-remote at scale.
- Multiple product surfaces or segments, often several capital rounds
  behind you, often profitable or near it.
- The board is real and institutional. Governance is a genuine function,
  not an afterthought.

### The founder's core question

> Can I scale without breaking the load-bearing parts of what made this
> work — the culture, the customer experience, the unit economics, the
> team's ability to make decisions without me?

The searches from mod-001 chapter 05 are mostly *closed*. The work is
**execution at scale**, and the risks are second-order: management
scaling, systems and controls, culture drift, capital-allocation choices
across a portfolio of internal bets.

### Typical focus

Active work:

- **Capital allocation** — with meaningful revenue and (usually) meaningful
  capital reserves, the CEO is picking among internal investments (new
  products, new segments, new geographies, M&A targets) rather than picking
  which single bet to make.
- **Management layers** — the first VP hires, the first C-suite build-out,
  the first geographic leads. The org exceeds the founder's individual
  span of control.
- **Systems and controls** — financial systems, HR systems, security and
  compliance programmes, real internal audit. What was informal at SERIES-A
  becomes a system at GROWTH.
- **Long-range planning** — 3-year plans that are neither science fiction
  nor useless. Real capital forecasts, real hiring plans, real product
  roadmaps.

Attention-drains that pretend to be work at GROWTH:

- Personally shipping code. (For most founders. There are exceptions.)
- Personally closing individual deals. (Ditto.)
- Founder-mode nostalgia — trying to run the company the way it ran at
  20 people when it now has 500.

The rule at GROWTH: **the founder's leverage is now the org, not the
individual work.** Every hour spent on individual-contributor work is an
hour not spent on the second-order problems only the founder can address.

## MATURE

### Signal you're here

- A **durable business** — profitable or reliably fund-able, with defensible
  moats, a real customer base, and multi-year visibility.
- **Optionality on outcomes** — the company can plausibly IPO, be acquired,
  stay private with secondaries, continue as an owner-operator, or transition
  to a professional CEO. The choice is being made, not forced.
- Governance is a serious function; committees are real; the board
  independently steers as well as advises.

### The founder's core question

> How do I sustain, govern, and steer the company to an outcome consistent
> with what the shareholders and the mission actually need?

By the mod-001 chapter 01 definition, MATURE is where the company has
stopped being a startup. The search is over; the company has found and
executes a repeatable, scalable business model. The founder's job shifts
from search to stewardship.

### Typical focus

Active work:

- **Governance and risk** — a real risk function, real board committees,
  real regulatory posture. Depth lives in
  `startup-operations-governance-curriculum`.
- **Succession** — founder-CEO transition planning, executive team
  succession, board refresh.
- **Exit / endgame** — M&A, secondaries, IPO, buyout, or continued
  independent operation as a deliberate strategy. Depth lives in
  `startup-exit-curriculum`.
- **Long-term capital allocation** — how the durable cash flow is deployed
  across dividends, buybacks, M&A, new bets. Look-alike to the Buffett /
  Berkshire framing mod-002 chapter 04 cites.

Attention-drains that pretend to be work at MATURE:

- Any activity that pretends the search is still open when it isn't.
- Any activity that pretends the search is closed when a specific
  business unit has regressed and is really operating at SEED again.

The rule at MATURE: **most of the org is executing; a small deliberate
part of the org is exploring the next thing.** The founder-CEO's job is
to make sure both are done well and neither smothers the other.

## The six-row summary

The full ladder, side by side:

| Stage | Signal | Founder's core question | Typical focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IDEA** | Hunch and a candidate problem; no company yet | Is this problem real and worth solving? | Customer discovery; first-principles problem framing |
| **PRE-SEED** | Incorporated (or about to be); building v0; some money | Can I get evidence a solution could work? | Solution scoping, MVP build, incorporation, founder agreements, first runway math, un-scalable hand-work |
| **SEED** | Early users/revenue; raising a first priced round; first hires | Can I find repeatable value and fund the PMF search? | Founder-led sales, runway management, cap table, first hires, first PMF signals |
| **SERIES-A** | PMF signals a fund will underwrite; institutional lead | Can I turn the working thing into a scalable machine? | Unit economics, GTM engine, org design, board governance, milestone-driven capital deployment |
| **GROWTH** | Repeatable growth; scaling headcount and spend | Can I scale without breaking the load-bearing parts? | Capital allocation, management layers, systems and controls, long-range planning |
| **MATURE** | Durable business; optionality on outcomes | How do I sustain, govern, and steer to an outcome? | Governance, succession, exit / endgame, long-term capital allocation |

## Two things to notice

**The premature column is longer than the active column at every stage.**
Look at IDEA. Twelve nodes on the mod-002 graph are premature; one is
active. Look at PRE-SEED. Ten are premature; three are active. It stays
that way — the point of the ladder is to *narrow* the founder's attention,
not to license working on everything at once.

**Stages compound; they don't replace.** A SEED-stage founder is still
doing customer discovery when the PMF signal weakens. A SERIES-A founder
is still running founder-led sales for the strategic accounts. A GROWTH
founder is still doing hiring calls for the C-suite. The stage tells you
what is *new* in the active column, not that the previous stage's work has
disappeared. Chapter 03 gives you the taxonomy for when a founder is stuck
running an earlier-stage activity long past the point where it should
have transitioned to a delegate.

## Summary

- Each stage has a specific **signal**, a specific **founder core
  question**, and a specific **typical focus**. Memorise the six-row
  table.
- The "typical focus" column is short at every stage — the whole point
  of the ladder is to narrow attention.
- Stages compound: later stages inherit some of earlier stages' work, but
  the *primary* active work changes at each rung.
- Any activity you can locate off the "typical focus" column of your
  current stage is a candidate for the mismatch teardown in the next
  chapter.
