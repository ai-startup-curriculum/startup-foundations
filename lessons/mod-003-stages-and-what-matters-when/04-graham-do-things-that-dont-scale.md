# Paul Graham — `Do Things That Don't Scale`

## Motivation

The mod-001 chapter 01 definition demands a business model that is
*scalable*. The stage ladder in chapter 02 of this module says that at IDEA
and PRE-SEED, the founder should be doing un-scalable hand-work with users
— personal onboarding, manual outreach, hand-crafted early customer
experiences.

Read side by side, those two statements look contradictory. If the endgame
is a scalable business, why is the earliest work un-scalable? Isn't that
just building a habit you'll have to unlearn?

Paul Graham's essay `Do Things That Don't Scale` (July 2013) is the
canonical answer to that question and the essay every IDEA-and-PRE-SEED
founder in this org is expected to have read. It is the reconciliation
between the scalability demand in the definition and the stage-appropriate
work at the bottom of the ladder.

This chapter is scaffolding. Read the primary essay:
<https://paulgraham.com/ds.html>.

## The core claim

Graham's argument, compressed to a sentence:

> Startups don't take off by themselves — the founders take them off, and
> the way founders take them off is by doing things that don't scale.

The received wisdom Graham is arguing against is that a "real" startup grows
because its product is good and its market is ready, and that founders who
have to *manually push* early customers out onto the runway are somehow
building a lesser business. Graham says that received wisdom is wrong, and
that the un-scalable manual push is *how essentially every well-known
successful startup got its first users*. The manual push isn't a compromise;
it's the work.

He gives several concrete examples in the essay. Some canonical ones (verify
the exact framing against the primary source):

- Airbnb's founders in New York, personally photographing early listings.
- Stripe's early "collison installation" — the co-founders getting real users
  set up in person rather than telling them "sign up on the site."
- The early Y Combinator batches recruited one at a time by Graham and Jessica
  Livingston personally reaching out to specific hackers.

The examples are load-bearing because they establish the pattern: the

there would have been no flywheel to scale.

## Why the un-scalable work is the right work at IDEA / PRE-SEED

Graham gives several reasons the un-scalable hand-work is not a compromise
but the actual load-bearing activity at the earliest stages. Three of them
are load-bearing for how you should read the chapter:

### 1. You learn more per user during the un-scalable phase than at any later stage.

When you personally onboard the first ten users, you see everything — every
place they get stuck, every phrase in your product that confuses them, every
adjacent problem they mention that you didn't know about, every price they
casually accept or reject, every workaround they used before you.

None of that signal survives a self-serve funnel. The self-serve funnel gives
you conversion rates and drop-off graphs — quantitative signal about
*aggregate* behaviour, at high volume, with the details compressed out. That
is valuable at SEED and beyond. At IDEA and PRE-SEED, you don't have the
volume for aggregate signal to mean anything, and the questions you need
answered are qualitative — *what is the problem, really?* — and require the
detail the un-scalable hand-work preserves.

This is why chapter 02 marks continued customer discovery as active work
right through PRE-SEED. The un-scalable hand-work is a customer-discovery
instrument, not a customer-acquisition compromise.

### 2. Un-scalable work is often the only way to break out of the cold-start problem.

Most valuable early-stage businesses have a chicken-and-egg problem: users
won't come to a product that no one uses; the product cannot get good until
users use it. The way through is manual: the founder brings the first users
personally, keeps them engaged personally, and produces the initial signal
of use that eventually attracts organic users. A landing page and an ad
campaign don't solve the cold-start problem. Hand-work does.

Marketplaces are the most extreme case (both sides need liquidity), but
almost every early product has some version of the problem — no
testimonials, no case studies, no reference customers, no reviews. The
un-scalable hand-work generates the first ones.

### 3. Delight has non-linear returns at the early stage.

Graham argues that at the earliest stage, aiming for *insanely great
experiences for a small number of users* pays back non-linearly, because
those users become the evangelists, the case studies, and the honest
feedback source. Trying to build a "scalable" merely-good experience for a
larger number of users at IDEA / PRE-SEED gives up the delight without
gaining the volume. It's the worst of both.

The un-scalable work is where delight lives — you can hand-craft an
experience that no self-serve product could match, precisely because
there are only ten users.

## The reconciliation with the scalability demand

The apparent contradiction resolves like this:

- The mod-001 definition says the *eventual business model* must be
  scalable — you cannot end up in a place where serving customer 10,000
  costs the same as serving customer 1.
- The un-scalable hand-work at IDEA / PRE-SEED does not make the eventual
  business model un-scalable. It makes the *early work* un-scalable.
  Those are different claims.
- The un-scalable hand-work is *how you learn what the scalable business
  model should be.* Skipping it does not accelerate the arrival at the
  scalable model; it prevents you from ever finding one.
- At SEED and beyond, the un-scalable hand-work is *replaced* by scalable
  systems — a self-serve funnel replaces founder-run onboarding, a sales
  team replaces founder-led selling, a support function replaces the
  founder-in-the-support-inbox. That replacement is a stage transition,
  not an abandonment.

The two ideas — scalability as the endgame, un-scalable hand-work as the
opening move — are not in tension. They are in *sequence*. Chapter 02's
ladder is that sequence.

## The three most common misreads of the essay

**Misread 1 — "I should just build a scalable process from day one to save
time later."** No. Graham's whole argument is that this is the mistake. The
scalable process you can build without user contact will be aimed at
non-existent user behaviour, and you'll rebuild it once you finally do the
hand-work. Save time by doing the hand-work now.

**Misread 2 — "The essay licenses skipping the product; I'll just do
services for the early users."** No. The hand-work is around the product,
not instead of it. Airbnb's founders photographed the listings for their
platform; Stripe's founders installed *their API* in person. If the
un-scalable hand-work is *unrelated* to the product, you are running a
consulting shop with startup branding — the trap named in mod-001 chapter
01, contrast 2.

**Misread 3 — "The essay is only about consumer / marketplace startups."**
No. The examples in the essay lean consumer / marketplace because that is
where the cold-start problem is most vivid, but the pattern applies
equally to B2B, developer tools, enterprise, and hardware. The B2B version
of "the founders photographed the listings" is the B2B founders manually
integrating the API into the first customer's stack, or hand-writing the
first ten sales-outreach emails, or personally running the demo for every
prospect.

## Where the un-scalable work ends

Graham does not dwell on the transition, but this chapter should. The
un-scalable hand-work is a **stage-bound** activity. Somewhere between
SEED and SERIES-A — the exact moment depends on the business — it stops
being the highest-leverage use of the founder's week and starts being a
shape-2 mismatch (not letting go) from chapter 03.

The signals that the un-scalable phase is ending:

- The manual work is producing evidence for the *same* insight over and
  over, rather than new insight. The learning rate is falling toward zero.
- Real demand is growing faster than the founder can personally serve. The
  bottleneck is *founder throughput*, not *product usefulness*.
- The un-scalable process has stabilised enough that you can describe it as
  a repeatable procedure — which is another way of saying it is now
  system-able.
- The founder is spending most of the week doing the manual work rather
  than talking to prospects for the next segment or thinking about the
  next constraint.

When those signals arrive, the correct move is to build the system —
onboarding flow, sales team, support function, self-serve funnel — that
replaces the hand-work. Continuing the hand-work past that point is
mismatch shape 2 (not letting go). Chapter 03 has the diagnosis.

## What the essay is not

Two things worth being clear about.

**It is not a licence for perpetual manual work.** The essay is about
*early-stage* founder behaviour. Building an entire company that never
graduates the founder from personally serving each customer is not the
Graham essay; it is a lifestyle business or a consulting practice —
mod-001 chapter 01, contrasts 2 and 3.

**It is not a substitute for the product being good.** The un-scalable
hand-work amplifies a product that is on its way to being good. It does
not compensate for a product that isn't. Graham is explicit that the
manual work is on top of a product the users would eventually love; the
manual work is what gets them to that love faster and with more signal.

## How this chapter connects to the rest of the module

Chapter 03's diagnosis of "an idea-stage founder optimising ad spend"
should now make sense: at IDEA, the correct current focus is the
un-scalable hand-work — personal conversations with candidate users, and
if you have a v0, personal onboarding of the first ones. Ad spend at
IDEA is a shape-1 mismatch (working ahead) *because* it substitutes
scalable behaviour for the un-scalable behaviour that would actually
produce learning.

Chapter 05's premature-scaling report will make the same point
empirically: companies that add scalable-looking infrastructure before
their stage warrants it are more likely to fail. Graham's essay is the
qualitative reasoning; the Startup Genome report is the empirical
evidence.

## Summary

- `Do Things That Don't Scale` is the canonical IDEA / PRE-SEED
  reconciliation of the scalability demand with the un-scalable
  hand-work the earliest stages require.
- The un-scalable work is right for three reasons: it maximises
  per-user learning, it breaks out of the cold-start problem, and
  delight has non-linear returns at low volume.
- Scalability is the *eventual* model; the un-scalable hand-work is
  *how you learn what that model should be*. They are in sequence,
  not in tension.
- The un-scalable phase ends when learning rate falls and founder
  throughput becomes the bottleneck; continuing past that point is
  chapter 03 mismatch shape 2 (not letting go).
- The essay is not a licence for perpetual manual work; it is a
  stage-bound instruction for the earliest stages.
