# The Stage-Fit Checklist

## Motivation

Chapters 01 through 05 gave you the vocabulary: the stage axis, the six-row
ladder, the three mismatch shapes, the Graham reconciliation for
IDEA / PRE-SEED, and the Startup Genome premature-scaling framing. This
chapter compresses all of it into an artifact you can point at any
candidate activity — a hire, a document, a tool purchase, a marketing
spend, an investor conversation, a feature build, a research read, a
board discussion — and decide whether it is stage-appropriate.

The checklist is deliberately short. It is meant to be run in five
minutes, before the activity starts, by a founder or by someone advising
one. If the check takes forty minutes, you have the wrong artifact.

The output is a **verdict** — proceed, defer, delegate, or replace — with
a **defence** the founder can commit to in writing. Committing the
verdict in writing is what turns the checklist from a mental habit into a
durable decision record that mod-004's decision log will inherit.

## The checklist

Six questions. Run them in order. The first one that is answered "no"
tells you the activity is a candidate for defer / delegate / replace and
the checklist tells you which.

### Question 1 — What node on the mod-002 graph does this activity live on?

If you cannot name a specific node, either the activity is off-graph
(personal-life items belong here; some are fine, some are not, but none
of them are stage-mismatched startup work), or your placement discipline
from mod-002 is weak. Retry the placement. If the answer is still "no
specific node," it is very likely the activity is not real founder work
— it is a vibes activity — and can be dropped.

If you can name the node, write it down. Every later question refers to
it.

### Question 2 — What is my current stage?

Not what you'd like it to be. Not what your cap table says. Not what your
last press release announced. Use the signal column from chapter 02 to
place the company on the ladder honestly. If you're not sure between two
adjacent rungs, pick the earlier one — the checklist is more conservative
that way and premature scaling (chapter 05) is the failure mode.

Write it down.

### Question 3 — Is the node from Q1 in the typical-focus column for the stage from Q2?

Refer to chapter 02's six-row table. If yes, the activity is
stage-appropriate on the map; skip to question 4 as a sanity check and
then to the verdict. If no, the activity is a candidate mismatch and the
diagnosis from chapter 03 kicks in:

- Is the node from a *later* stage's focus? → shape 1 (working ahead) →
  verdict candidate: **defer**.
- Is the node from an *earlier* stage's focus that has now moved to
  delegation? → shape 2 (not letting go) → verdict candidate:
  **delegate**.
- Is the node on a different *branch* of the graph than where the
  current stage's binding constraint lives? → shape 3 (wrong branch) →
  verdict candidate: **replace** — with the specific
  binding-constraint activity that should have taken the slot.

### Question 4 — Does this activity premature-scale any Startup Genome dimension?

Refer to chapter 05. Even an activity on the right node can be a
premature-scaling move on one of the dimensions (team, product,
customer, business model, financials). Ask:

- **Team** — does this activity add scale-shape to the team (a
  specialist role, a manager layer, a formal committee) beyond what
  the current stage warrants?
- **Product** — does this activity add product surface (a new module,
  an integrations marketplace, a full admin console) before the
  minimum product is being used?
- **Customer** — does this activity aggressively acquire users before
  retention is proven?
- **Business model** — does this activity lock in pricing, packaging,
  or channel structure before the pricing has been validated by real
  customers?
- **Financials** — does this activity spend or commit capital at a
  rate that presumes a later stage's revenue?

If any answer is yes, mark the activity as a **premature-scaling
candidate** and hold it against the constraint check in question 5.

### Question 5 — Is this activity contributing to the founder's core question for the current stage?

Refer to chapter 02. Each stage has a single core question. Ask: if this
activity produces its intended output, does the output move the core
question closer to being answered with evidence?

- IDEA — does the output produce evidence for or against "is this
  problem real and worth solving?"
- PRE-SEED — does the output produce evidence for or against "can any
  solution to this problem work?"
- SEED — does the output produce evidence for or against "is there
  repeatable value; can I fund the PMF search?"
- SERIES-A — does the output produce evidence for or against "is
  there a scalable machine here?"
- GROWTH — does the output help scale without breaking the
  load-bearing parts?
- MATURE — does the output steward the business toward an outcome?

If the answer is a clear yes, the activity has stage-appropriate
motivation, not just a stage-appropriate location. If the answer is
"maybe" or "indirectly," the activity is probably not the
highest-leverage use of the founder's time even if it isn't strictly
mismatched.

### Question 6 — Who is doing this — and should they?

The final check. Even a stage-appropriate activity is a mismatch if the
*wrong person* is doing it. Ask:

- Is this activity currently in the *founder's* hands? If so, is it
  chapter 02's typical focus for the current stage, or is it a shape-2
  (not-letting-go) mismatch where the founder should have delegated by
  now?
- If the founder should not personally be doing it, who should? Do
  they exist in the org? If not, is the correct move to *hire the
  person* or *defer the activity until the person exists*?

This question catches the case where the *what* is fine but the *who*
is wrong. A SERIES-A founder personally running the ATS pipeline for
every open role is a shape-2 mismatch on the hiring activity, even
though hiring is stage-appropriate.

## The verdict form

The output of the checklist is one of four verdicts, each with a
defence line:

- **Proceed** — activity is stage-appropriate, does not premature-scale
  any dimension, contributes to the current stage's core question, and
  the right person is doing it. Verdict form:
  > *Proceed. Activity lives at [node]. Current stage [X]. Node is in
  > the typical focus. Contributes to core question by [one line]. Right
  > person is [role].*

- **Defer** — activity is later-stage. Not wrong; not now. Verdict form:
  > *Defer. Activity lives at [node]. Current stage [X]. Node is
  > typical focus at [later stage Y]. Revisit when [specific signal of
  > stage transition]. Do [current stage's typical focus activity]
  > instead this week.*

- **Delegate** — activity is stage-appropriate but the wrong person is
  doing it. Verdict form:
  > *Delegate. Activity lives at [node]. Current stage [X]. Founder is
  > currently doing it. Should be [role]. Hire / promote / hand off
  > by [when]; founder covers only [exception cases].*

- **Replace** — activity is off the current stage's typical focus or
  is on the wrong branch for the binding constraint; do
  the correct activity instead. Verdict form:
  > *Replace. Activity lives at [node]. Current stage [X]. Node is
  > premature / stale / off-branch because [one line]. Correct current
  > focus is [specific stage-appropriate activity]. Do that.*

Each verdict is committed to the mod-004 decision log with a
timestamp so that a future review — or a future you — can look back
and see whether the verdict held.

## Worked example — a real candidate activity

Take a candidate activity: **"Retain a Big Four accounting firm to
review our transfer-pricing exposure across our (planned) EU and US
entities."** The founder is at PRE-SEED, US-incorporated, no
customers, no EU entity, and no revenue.

- Q1 — Node: this lives at the Governance / Legal-Ops region of the
  corporate-structure branch. Specifically, cross-border tax
  structuring is a sub-node of that region.
- Q2 — Current stage: PRE-SEED (no customers, no revenue, no round;
  the founder is v0-building).
- Q3 — In typical focus? No. Cross-border tax structuring is a
  GROWTH-or-later focus, not a PRE-SEED one. Shape 1 (working
  ahead).
- Q4 — Premature-scaling? Yes, on the *team / financials* dimension
  — retaining a Big Four firm on cross-border tax is a GROWTH-level
  commitment of both team-shape (specialist advisors) and financials
  (Big Four fees). Extreme mismatch on the financial dimension
  given no revenue.
- Q5 — Contributes to core question ("can I get evidence a solution
  could work?"). No, not at all.
- Q6 — Who's doing it? Founder is proposing to retain the firm.
  Answer: no one should be doing it at PRE-SEED.
- **Verdict: Defer.** Revisit when the company has cross-border
  revenue material enough to warrant transfer-pricing analysis. Do
  v0 build with users this week instead. Committed with today's
  date; next review at SERIES-A close.

That is what the artifact looks like when it works.

## Anti-patterns the checklist catches

**"But everyone at our stage is doing it."** Doesn't matter. Chapter
05's whole point is that the reason many companies at your stage die
is because they're doing this. The reference-class is failing; the
correct question is what the *surviving* companies at your stage did.

**"Our investor wants us to do it."** A material input, but not the
final answer. If the investor is asking for a shape-1 activity
(working ahead), the correct response is to have the conversation
about *why* it is premature and what will convince them the deferral
is right. Sometimes the investor is right and the founder has
mis-placed themselves on the ladder; sometimes the investor is
projecting a later-stage playbook onto your company. Use the
checklist as the frame for the conversation.

**"We can do it in parallel; it doesn't cost anything."** It does
cost something. It costs founder attention — the scarcest resource
in a startup. If it takes even four hours per week of founder time,
that is four hours not spent on the current-stage core question.
Multiply by a few weeks and the cost is material.

**"We already started; the sunk cost is high."** Chapter 05's mechanism
of failure is precisely this — the sunk-cost momentum keeps a
premature-scaling activity alive after it should have been stopped. The
checklist re-runs at any point; the correct verdict at week four is
often the same as the correct verdict at week zero, and the sunk cost
does not change it.

## When the checklist gives a wrong answer

The checklist is a heuristic, not a proof. Sometimes it says "defer"
and the activity really is stage-appropriate for reasons the checklist
doesn't capture — regulatory deadlines that don't wait for stage
readiness (data-privacy compliance ahead of a specific launch),
governance moves that fix a specific bug in the corporate-structure
branch (a founder departure that requires a real severance framework
right now), or one-off strategic accounts that require a specific
un-scalable behaviour a stage past when it would normally be delegated.

The escape hatch is explicit. If you override the checklist verdict,
you record the override with:

- Which check the override applies to (Q1 through Q6).
- Why the check does not apply to this specific case (one paragraph).
- What signal would tell you the override was wrong.
- When you will re-run the checklist to see if the exception has
  passed.

The override is not free — it is a decision on the record, and
downstream you can see whether the override was warranted. Most
overrides in practice are wrong (the sunk-cost story from above is
the most common), so the record is what keeps the founder honest.

## How the checklist gets used every week

The checklist is not a one-time diagnostic. It is a **weekly
operating tool** that connects mod-003 to mod-004:

- Every Monday, when you plan the week (mod-004's cadence), each
  candidate activity in the plan gets a checklist verdict. Anything
  that verdicts as **defer / delegate / replace** does not stay in
  the plan without an explicit override.
- Every Friday, when you review the week, each activity that
  actually consumed founder time gets a *retrospective* checklist
  read. Anything that consumed time but should have verdicted
  differently is caught in the retrospective.
- Every quarter, when the stage may have shifted, the whole
  checklist gets a re-baseline: re-answer Q2 (current stage) and
  see how many recurring activities need to be re-verdicted.

That's what makes the stage vocabulary from this module operational
rather than theoretical.

## Summary

- The checklist is six questions in order: node, stage, typical
  focus, premature-scaling check, core-question contribution, right
  person.
- The verdict is one of four: **proceed**, **defer**, **delegate**,
  **replace** — each with a specific defence-line format.
- Overrides are allowed but recorded with a reason, a falsification
  signal, and a re-check date.
- The checklist is a weekly operating tool used with mod-004's
  cadence, not a one-time diagnostic.
- Every anti-pattern that the checklist catches ("everyone's doing
  it," "the investor wants it," "we can parallelise," "we've
  started") is a version of the failure mode Startup Genome
  documented in chapter 05.
