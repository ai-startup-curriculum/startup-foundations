# Stage-Mismatched Activity — What It Looks Like and Why It Kills

## Motivation

Chapter 02 gave you the six-row ladder — a signal, a core question, and a
typical-focus set for each stage. Chapter 06 will turn that into a checklist
you can apply to any candidate activity. This chapter sits between them and
does the job the ladder alone cannot: it teaches you to *see* stage-mismatched
activity in the wild, name the shape of the mismatch, and diagnose the correct
current focus that should have replaced it.

The label "stage-mismatched activity" is doing a lot of work. It does not mean
"the founder is doing something wrong." It means the founder is doing something
that *would be right at a different stage* — usually a later stage — but is
premature or delegated at the current one. The result is a founder who feels
busy and sophisticated while the load-bearing question of the current stage
goes unanswered. Every dollar of runway spent on a stage-mismatched activity
is a dollar not spent on the stage-appropriate one, and the runway does not
lengthen in sympathy.

Stage-mismatched activity is not a rare pathology. It is the single most
common way over-schooled founders waste their runway. Naming it is the first
line of defence.

## The three shapes of stage-mismatched activity

Not every mismatch is the same shape. There are three that cover almost every
case in the wild.

### Shape 1 — Working ahead

The founder is doing work that would be correct at a *later* stage, but is
premature at the current one. The most common shape; also the one that
attracts the most positive reinforcement (people admire it because it looks
sophisticated), so it is the hardest to catch in yourself.

Signature: the founder can defend the work as "we'll need this eventually."
That defence is a red flag. Everything on the graph will eventually be
needed. The question the stage is asking is *which of the eventual things
matter this week?*

Concrete examples:

- **A seed-stage founder building a compensation-committee charter.**
  Compensation committees are a real Series-A-or-later governance artifact.
  At SEED, with a three-person board and five employees, the committee has
  nothing to govern and no one to govern it. Building the charter now
  produces a document that will be rewritten from scratch when a real board
  and a real HR function exist. The current focus that should have replaced
  it: founder-led sales, first-hire calibration, and cap-table hygiene.

- **A pre-seed founder studying multinational transfer pricing.**
  Transfer pricing is a real problem for companies with cross-border
  intercompany transactions and material cross-border revenue — typically
  GROWTH-stage or later. At PRE-SEED, with no revenue in one country let
  alone two, the topic is intellectually engaging and operationally
  useless. The current focus that should have replaced it: v0 build,
  problem-search closure, first runway math.

- **An idea-stage founder optimising ad spend.** Ad-spend optimisation
  is a real SEED-through-GROWTH activity. At IDEA, there is no product
  to advertise, no customer to acquire, and no channel to optimise. The
  spend produces vanity clicks and destroys signal. The current focus
  that should have replaced it: customer discovery conversations —
  actual ones, with actual humans in the target segment.

Working ahead is dangerous because it feels productive. The work exists, the
work looks like real founder work, the work would be right *someday*. The
diagnosis is not "you did a bad job of it" — it is "you did an excellent job
of the wrong stage's work."

### Shape 2 — Not letting go

The founder is doing work that was correct at an *earlier* stage but should
now be delegated. The company has moved on; the founder has not.

Signature: the founder can defend the work as "I'm the only one who can do
this." That defence is a red flag as of SERIES-A. If it is still true at
SERIES-A, the founder has not built the machine the stage requires.

Concrete examples:

- **A Series-A CEO personally closing every deal.** Founder-led sales is
  the right SEED-stage motion. At SERIES-A, the founder's job is to hire
  and build the sales *function*, not to remain the primary salesperson.
  A founder still closing every deal at SERIES-A is stalling the machine
  build-out — and the deals will fall off a cliff when the founder is
  pulled into fundraising for the B round.

- **A GROWTH CEO personally reviewing every hire's offer letter.** Making
  every hire well is a right SEED / SERIES-A activity. At GROWTH, with
  hundreds of employees, the CEO signing every letter creates a bottleneck
  and a false intimacy. The right work is a hiring *system* — leveling,
  compensation bands, delegated approval authority — that the founder
  designs but does not personally operate.

- **A SEED founder still writing all the code.** Absolutely appropriate
  at PRE-SEED and IDEA. At SEED, with the beginning of a real customer
  base and the beginning of runway pressure, the founder writing all the
  code is often the reason the first hire keeps getting pushed off. The
  right move is to hire the engineer who lets the founder be the operator
  they are becoming.

Not letting go is dangerous because it feels loyal. The founder is doing the
work that got them here; the work is quality work; letting go feels like
abandoning the thing they built. The diagnosis is not "you're bad at it" — it
is "you shouldn't be the one doing it anymore, and the fact that you still
are is holding back the stage transition."

### Shape 3 — Working the wrong branch

The founder is spending time in the *wrong branch of the mod-002 graph* for
the current stage — usually the corporate-structure or economics branch when
the current stage's action is on the customer-facing branch, or vice versa.

Signature: the founder can name the branch they're working in, but cannot
name why it is the *binding constraint* right now. "I'm doing fundraising
prep" is a branch. "I'm doing fundraising prep because runway ends in 4
months and no customer growth will close the gap in time" is a
constraint-directed choice. The former is often mismatched; the latter is
what the constraint requires.

Concrete examples:

- **A PRE-SEED founder spending weeks on a detailed 5-year financial
  model.** The economics branch is important. At PRE-SEED, with no
  customers and no pricing evidence, the 5-year model is fiction — the
  assumptions underneath every cell will be replaced by real data in the
  next 12 months. The customer-facing branch is where the binding
  constraint is. The two-line runway math from chapter 02 is enough
  economics work at PRE-SEED.

- **A SEED founder spending a month building a formal board-committee
  structure.** The corporate-structure branch is important. At SEED, with a
  three-seat board (typically two co-founders and one seed investor),
  committees are theatre. The customer-facing branch — repeatable early
  sales — is the binding constraint. The board-committee build-out
  belongs at SERIES-A when a real board arrives.

- **A GROWTH founder spending most of their week in the customer-facing
  branch, hand-selling to every strategic prospect.** The customer-facing
  branch is important. At GROWTH, the binding constraint is usually in
  the corporate-structure branch (governance, org design at scale, board
  and shareholder communication) or the economics branch (capital
  allocation across a portfolio of internal bets). The individual
  strategic deal is a fair investment of a few days a quarter; a majority
  of the week is a regression to SERIES-A behaviour.

Working the wrong branch is dangerous because both branches are on the
graph — the work is not obviously "not real work." The diagnosis is about
*allocation of attention across the branches*, not about whether the
individual activity is legitimate in isolation.

## A four-step diagnosis

When you look at a candidate activity and suspect it is stage-mismatched,
run the same four steps every time.

**Step 1 — Locate the activity on the mod-002 graph.** Name the node it
belongs to. If you cannot place it, either the activity is genuinely
off-graph (fine; some founder activities are personal — sleep, exercise,
therapy — and belong on no branch) or your placement discipline from
mod-002 is weak and you should retry the exercise from that module.

**Step 2 — Locate the founder on the stage ladder.** Use the signal column
from chapter 02. Do not use vibes; do not use the founder's self-report;
use the observable signals. If you're honest, the founder is often at a
different stage than the one they claim.

**Step 3 — Ask: is the node from step 1 in the "typical focus" column of
the stage from step 2?** If yes, the activity is stage-appropriate and
you're done. If no, the activity is a candidate mismatch.

**Step 4 — Classify the mismatch as one of the three shapes.** Is the
node from a *later* stage's focus (working ahead — shape 1)? An
*earlier* stage's focus that should now be delegated (not letting go —
shape 2)? Or on a different *branch* than the one where the stage's
binding constraint lives (working the wrong branch — shape 3)? Then
name **what the correct current focus is** — the specific
stage-appropriate activity that should have replaced the mismatched
one.

The output of the four steps is a short diagnosis:

> The activity is [node]. The founder is at [stage]. That node is [premature /
> stale / off-branch] at this stage; specifically it is [shape 1 / 2 / 3].
> The correct current focus is [specific node from this stage's typical
> focus column].

You will run this four-step diagnosis over and over — for every candidate
week's plan, for every hire discussion, for every tool purchase, for every
piece of external advice. It should become mechanical.

## Why stage-mismatched work is not just "inefficient"

It is tempting to treat stage-mismatched activity as merely wasteful — an
efficiency problem, not a survival problem. That is wrong. The reason to
take it seriously is that a startup runs on a finite budget of two things,
and both are actively consumed by mismatched work:

**Runway.** Every hour, every dollar, and every mental cycle spent on
stage-mismatched work is not spent on stage-appropriate work, and the
stage-appropriate work is what closes the current stage's core question.
Runway does not extend to make room for the misallocation.

**Attention.** A founder's most scarce non-financial resource. Every
context switch to a mismatched activity is a context switch away from the
core question. Ten weeks spent building a Series-A-shaped compensation
committee at SEED is not just ten weeks wasted; it is ten weeks the seed
question stayed open when it might have closed.

Chapter 05 of this module reads the Startup Genome premature-scaling
report as the empirical anchor for this argument: companies that
consistently scale beyond their stage are much more likely to fail. The
mechanism is that mismatched work eats the budget the stage-appropriate
work needed.

## The special case — un-scalable hand-work at IDEA / PRE-SEED

The mod-001 chapter 01 definition demands a *scalable* business model
eventually. That fact is often misread at IDEA and PRE-SEED as: "so I
must not do anything un-scalable now." The result is founders who refuse
to do the hand-work — personally onboarding early users, hand-crafting
first campaigns, going door to door — because the hand-work "doesn't
scale."

This is a serious mis-reading of both the definition and the stage
ladder. Chapter 04 of this module is entirely about that mis-reading —
Paul Graham's `Do Things That Don't Scale` is the canonical
reconciliation. The short version: the un-scalable hand-work at IDEA /
PRE-SEED is *how you learn the thing that eventually becomes scalable.*
Skipping it does not make you scale sooner; it makes you never learn
enough to build a scalable thing at all.

For this chapter's diagnosis: un-scalable hand-work at IDEA and PRE-SEED
is **not** a stage mismatch. It is the correct current focus. Chapter
04 says why in detail.

## Two failure modes when diagnosing

**Failure mode 1 — diagnosing every un-loved activity as a mismatch.**
Not every activity you dislike is stage-mismatched. Some are just hard.
Cold customer outreach at IDEA is stage-appropriate and it is hard. Do
not use the mismatch taxonomy as an excuse to skip work you don't want to
do.

**Failure mode 2 — diagnosing a legitimate rare exception as a mismatch.**
Some Series-A CEOs personally close some deals because those deals are
strategic and the customer explicitly requires it. That is not shape 2
mismatched; it is a legitimate exception. The diagnosis is about the
majority of the founder's week, not the individual event. Ask whether the
mismatched shape is *the pattern* or a defensible one-off.

## Summary

- Stage-mismatched activity is the single most common way over-schooled
  founders waste runway.
- Three shapes cover almost every case: **working ahead** (later-stage
  work now), **not letting go** (earlier-stage work that should be
  delegated), **working the wrong branch** (wrong branch of the graph
  for the current stage's binding constraint).
- The four-step diagnosis: locate the activity on the graph, locate the
  founder on the ladder, ask if the node is in the stage's typical
  focus, classify the mismatch and name the correct current focus.
- Mismatched work is not merely inefficient — it consumes the two
  resources (runway, attention) that the stage-appropriate work
  requires.
- Un-scalable hand-work at IDEA / PRE-SEED is **not** a mismatch;
  chapter 04 explains why in detail.
