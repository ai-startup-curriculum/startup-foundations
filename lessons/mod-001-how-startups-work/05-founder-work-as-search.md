# The Founder's Job Is a Search

## Motivation

Chapter 01 defined a startup as an organization *designed to search* for a
repeatable and scalable business model. Chapter 04 introduced the two operating
traditions built to structure that search. This chapter unpacks the "search"
word itself — because if the founder's job is fundamentally a search, then
"what am I searching for right now?" is the single most important question at
any given moment, and most founder confusion is a version of not being able to
answer it clearly.

The chapter has one goal: to give you a small, load-bearing vocabulary — the
**five searches** — that lets you name, at any moment, which search is currently
active and which artifacts belong to it.

## The five searches

Blank's definition — "searching for a repeatable and scalable business model" —
uses the word "search" in the singular. In practice, that top-level search
decomposes into five sub-searches that a founder runs, in rough order, over
the life of the startup. They overlap and re-open, but the sequence and the
priority they take over each other are stable enough to name.

### Search 1 — The problem search

**Question you're answering.** Is there a real, unresolved, painful problem
that a specific group of people has today, and is it worth solving?

**What "found" looks like.** You can name the target user in a sentence, you
can describe the problem in the user's own words (not yours), you have
evidence — from real conversations, not from your imagination — that the user
recognizes the problem and would spend time or money to remove it. A rough
target size for the user segment.

**Failure modes.** Building a solution before you can articulate the problem.
Assuming the problem is real because you would want the solution yourself.
Confusing "people don't like X" with "people will pay to remove X."

**Owning tradition.** Blank's Customer Discovery. The chapter 03 essay `The
18 Mistakes` names several problem-layer mistakes explicitly.

### Search 2 — The solution search

**Question you're answering.** Given a validated problem, what is the
minimum-shape solution that a user would actually adopt, use, and prefer over
their current workaround?

**What "found" looks like.** Real users using an MVP repeatedly, choosing it
over the alternative they used before, and giving qualitative signal that it's
solving the problem. Not necessarily paying yet.

**Failure modes.** Building the polished vision instead of the minimum
solution. Building the technically interesting thing rather than the thing
users need. Falling in love with the solution before confirming the problem.

**Owning tradition.** Ries's Build-Measure-Learn with MVPs. This is where
Ries's contribution is sharpest — the discipline of the smallest possible test.

### Search 3 — The market search

**Question you're answering.** Beyond the individual users, is there a
*market* — a coherent segment large enough, reachable enough, and homogeneous
enough that a repeatable business can be built inside it?

**What "found" looks like.** You can describe the initial beachhead segment
precisely (an ICP — Ideal Customer Profile). You have evidence that customers
inside that segment behave similarly enough that the same product, pricing,
and message work across many of them. You have a plausible expansion story
beyond the beachhead.

**Failure modes.** Serving customers who happen to be nearby rather than
customers who are part of a coherent segment. Confusing "we have ten paying
customers" with "we have a market" when the ten customers have nothing in
common. Naming the market so broadly ("all developers") that it can't be
targeted.

**Owning tradition.** Blank's Customer Validation. Depth lives in
`startup-product-gtm-curriculum`; Foundations owns the position of the
question.

### Search 4 — The business-model search

**Question you're answering.** For this problem, this solution, and this
market, is there a *business model* — a pricing, packaging, and sales-motion
combination — that produces unit economics that can support a real business?

**What "found" looks like.** You know what to charge, how to package, which
sales motion works (self-serve, sales-led, product-led-growth, channel), and
you can point to at least early evidence that CAC is meaningfully lower than
LTV, that gross margin is healthy for the model's type, and that payback is
tolerable.

**Failure modes.** Charging nothing "to grow" and never proving anyone would
pay. Charging enterprise prices with a self-serve motion. Building a great
product with terrible margins. Assuming the price you *want* to charge is the
price customers will *accept*.

**Owning tradition.** This is where the customer-facing branch of the graph
(PMF → GTM → Sales) meets the economics branch (Unit Economics). Foundations
introduces the vocabulary in mod-005. Depth lives in
`startup-product-gtm-curriculum` and `startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum`.

### Search 5 — The team search

**Question you're answering.** Do you have — or can you assemble — the team
that can actually execute this business at the size it can grow to?

**What "found" looks like.** Co-founders who are complementary, aligned, and
committed. Early hires who make the founders faster rather than slower. Clear
accountability for each search that is currently open. Founders who can
still work together after a hard week.

**Failure modes.** Picking a co-founder because they were available rather
than because they were right. Solo founding when the work needs two.
Hiring specialists before there's a business to specialize inside. Refusing to
fire an early hire who has become the bottleneck.

**Owning tradition.** Foundations covers the founder-agreement basics in
mod-004; hiring, comp, and management depth is in
`startup-operations-governance-curriculum`.

## The searches are not strictly sequential

The five searches are ordered by *priority* — you cannot productively run a
business-model search before you have a validated problem — but they are not
strictly sequential. In a working startup, several are usually active at once,
and it is normal for a downstream search to re-open an upstream one.

Concrete examples:

- You have a validated problem (search 1) and are searching for the solution
  (search 2). While iterating on the MVP, users describe the problem
  differently than you did. Search 1 re-opens — the problem was subtly
  wrong.
- You have a working solution (searches 1–2) and a business-model search
  underway (search 4). Every customer wants a different price. Either the
  market (search 3) is not as coherent as you thought — re-open — or the
  packaging is wrong.
- The team search (search 5) is always open. Founders come and go, key hires
  don't work out, an incoming senior person requires the org shape to
  change. Team never "closes."

This is normal, not a symptom of failure. What is a symptom of failure is
running the *wrong* search at the wrong time — for instance, doing team
build-out (search 5) at scale while the problem (search 1) is still not
validated, or doing pricing experiments (search 4) before the solution
(search 2) is stable enough to price.

## Each stage is a different search

The stage ladder (`STARTUP_STAGES.md`) is aligned with the search order:

| Stage | Primary search | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| **IDEA** | Problem search (1) | Team search (5) |
| **PRE-SEED** | Solution search (2) | Problem search (1), Team (5) |
| **SEED** | Market search (3) + early business-model (4) | Team (5) |
| **SERIES-A** | Business-model search (4) at scale | Market expansion, team |
| **GROWTH** | Execution — searches largely closed; scale-up begins | Team, capital allocation |
| **MATURE** | Not a startup anymore | — |

This table is one of the most useful heuristics in the module because it
answers the founder's most common question — *what should I be spending my
week on?* — in a stage-aware way. If you're at PRE-SEED and you're spending
the week on business-model experiments (search 4), you are almost certainly
skipping ahead. Stage-mismatched work is one of the topics of mod-003.

## The consequence for how the week is spent

If the founder's job is a search, then the operating loop in mod-004 — decide,
allocate, communicate, measure — is really a loop of *managing which search
gets the founder's attention this week and what evidence would close it.*

The single question that keeps a founder honest, repeated weekly:

> Which of the five searches is currently binding? What evidence would tell me
> that search is complete? What am I doing this week to gather that evidence?

If you cannot answer those three questions, the week is being spent on motion
rather than search — and motion without search is exactly how a startup burns
runway without progress.

## Summary

- Blank's "search" decomposes into five sub-searches: problem, solution,
  market, business model, team.
- The searches are priority-ordered but not strictly sequential; downstream
  searches routinely re-open upstream ones.
- Each stage of a startup is a different search (mostly): IDEA is problem,
  PRE-SEED is solution, SEED is market and early business-model, SERIES-A is
  business model at scale, GROWTH is execution.
- The founder's weekly question: which search is binding, what evidence would
  close it, what am I doing this week to get that evidence?
