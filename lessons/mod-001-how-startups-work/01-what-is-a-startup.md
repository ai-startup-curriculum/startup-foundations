# What Is a Startup — A Precise Definition

## Motivation

"Startup" is one of the most misused words in business writing. Bakeries, boutique
consultancies, indie SaaS shops, biotech research spinouts, and lifestyle Etsy
stores are all routinely called startups. Almost none of them are.

The word is used loosely because the label is desirable — startups sound
ambitious, well-funded, and important. Loose usage is fine in casual conversation
and lethal when you use it to decide what to work on, how to fund it, who to
hire, and what "good" looks like. Nearly every mistake in the later modules of
this curriculum traces back to a founder applying startup logic to a business
that isn't one, or applying small-business logic to something that is.

This chapter installs a precise, load-bearing definition and four contrasts that
you can apply in under a minute to any candidate business.

## The definition

The definition this curriculum uses is Steve Blank's:

> **A startup is a temporary organization designed to search for a repeatable and
> scalable business model.**

Every word in that sentence is doing work. Break it down:

- **Temporary** — a startup is not the end state. It exists to *become* something
  else: either a company that has found its model and can execute against it, or a
  failed experiment that shuts down. Any organization that intends to remain in
  "startup mode" indefinitely is not a startup; it is a small business that has
  adopted startup aesthetics.
- **Organization** — a startup is a coordinated group of people, not a solo
  side-project or a one-off consulting engagement. Solo builders can absolutely
  create startups, but the *organization* they are trying to bring into being is
  the point.
- **Designed to search** — the primary activity of a startup is search, not
  execution. It does not yet know *who* the customer is, *what* they'll pay for,
  *how* the product should be sold, or *whether* the unit economics work.
  Everything about how it staffs, plans, and spends should reflect that it is
  still finding out. When it stops searching and starts executing, it has
  graduated from being a startup.
- **A repeatable business model** — the same value proposition can be sold to the
  next customer, and the one after that, without a bespoke redesign each time. If
  every deal is a snowflake, there is no repeatable model — there is a
  consulting practice.
- **A scalable business model** — you can grow revenue without a proportional
  growth in cost. Each additional unit of output costs less than the last, or at
  worst the same. If serving the ten-thousandth customer costs the same as
  serving the first, there is no scalability — there is a job.

Eric Ries later added the phrase **under conditions of extreme uncertainty** to
the same definition. This is not a stylistic flourish. It is the reason startups
need their own methodology — you cannot plan your way through uncertainty; you
have to learn your way through it. That is what the two operating traditions in
chapter 04 are about.

Put together, this is the working definition for the rest of the curriculum:

> A startup is a temporary organization searching for a repeatable and scalable
> business model, under conditions of extreme uncertainty.

## The four contrasts

Any candidate business either fits this definition or it doesn't. The most useful
way to sharpen the definition is by contrasting it against the four things a
startup is most often confused with.

### Contrast 1 — startup vs. small business

A **small business** — a restaurant, a plumbing outfit, a local law firm, a
neighborhood coffee shop — knows what it is going to do, who it is going to sell
to, and roughly what it is going to charge. It is not searching for a business
model; it is executing an already-known one. The playbook exists — other
restaurants, other plumbers, other law firms already work — and the
entrepreneur's job is to execute it well in a specific place at a specific price
point.

Small businesses can be excellent businesses. They are often more profitable per
founder-hour than startups. But they are not designed to grow fast, they do not
need venture capital, they do not scale non-linearly, and applying startup
tooling to them (SAFEs, option pools, board seats, hockey-stick growth targets)
is not sophistication — it is category confusion.

The single sharpest test: **is there already a proven business model to copy?**
If yes, it is a small business. If the founder must find the model, it is a
startup.

### Contrast 2 — startup vs. consulting project

A **consulting project** is bespoke value delivery. The client has a problem,
you have a skill, and you agree a scope and a price to solve their problem
specifically for them. There is no repeatable product; each engagement is
priced, scoped, and delivered separately.

Consulting can look like a startup from the outside — you have customers, you
have revenue, you have deliverables — but the model is fundamentally *linear in
labor*. To double revenue, you roughly need to double the labor. There is no
scalable business model to search for because the whole enterprise is designed
around a non-scalable one.

Many failed startups are secretly consultancies that never noticed. If your
"product" is really a wrapper around expert humans, if each customer requires a
custom implementation, if churn is high whenever a customer stops needing that
specific help — you may be running a consulting firm with startup branding.

The single sharpest test: **can the tenth customer be served without hand-work
from a founder?** If no, you are consulting.

### Contrast 3 — startup vs. lifestyle business

A **lifestyle business** exists to fund and enable a particular life for its
owners — location freedom, a comfortable income, a schedule that respects
family, low stress, control. It may be highly profitable and highly successful
by its own goals. It is deliberately not designed to grow beyond the size where
those lifestyle constraints break.

There is nothing wrong with lifestyle businesses. Many are excellent. But they
have a different objective function than a startup — they optimize for stability
and quality of life, not for scale and outsized outcome. A startup by contrast
is aiming for an outcome large enough to justify the risk of failure that comes
with searching under uncertainty; a lifestyle business is deliberately trading
that away for control.

Signals you're running a lifestyle business: hiring is deliberately capped;
outside capital is refused or avoided; growth is throttled to protect margins or
work-life shape; the founder's stated goal is a specific level of stable income
rather than a specific size of outcome.

The single sharpest test: **is the owner willing to sacrifice quality of life
for a larger outcome?** If no, it is a lifestyle business.

### Contrast 4 — startup vs. research program

A **research program** is a systematic investigation into an open question,
usually in a university, a national lab, or an R&D division. It aims to produce
knowledge — papers, patents, techniques, understanding. It is measured in
citations, publications, novelty, and rigor. It is often funded on multi-year
grant cycles by governments or foundations.

Research shares one thing with startups: extreme uncertainty. It differs on
everything else. Research does not need to find customers, does not need to
find a scalable business model, and is not evaluated on whether anyone would
pay for the output. Many researchers can pursue a promising thread for years
even if the eventual application is unknown. A startup cannot; it runs out of
money.

Deep-tech startups are the muddy case — a startup built around a novel piece of
science looks like a research program in its early years. The line is drawn on
intent: is the goal to build a business that sells the output at scale, or is
the goal to advance the science? If the goal is the science, and a business is
"maybe someday," it is a research program. If the goal is the business, and the
science is the means, it is a startup — and everything from chapter 04 applies.

The single sharpest test: **is the goal a scalable revenue-producing business,
or is the goal knowledge?** Startups are always the former.

## A quick diagnostic

Apply these four questions in order to any candidate business:

1. Is there already a proven business model to copy? → small business.
2. Does serving customer #10 still require founder hand-work? → consulting.
3. Is the founder trading outcome size for lifestyle? → lifestyle business.
4. Is the goal knowledge rather than a scalable revenue business? → research
   program.

Only if the answer to all four is "no" is the thing in front of you actually a
startup.

## Why this matters for the rest of the curriculum

The three-axis model in the repo root — functional pillars, role pathways, stages
— all assume this definition. The stage ladder (IDEA → PRE-SEED → SEED → SERIES-A
→ GROWTH → MATURE) is a ladder of *search progress*: how much of the business
model has been found. The dependency graph in mod-002 organises knowledge by
which parts of the model must be found first. The founder-numbers in mod-005
(runway, burn, growth) are the instrumentation for whether the search will
finish before the money runs out.

A small business, a consulting project, a lifestyle business, and a research
program each need real, serious operating knowledge — but almost none of it is
in this curriculum. That is by design.

## Summary

- The definition: a temporary organization searching for a repeatable and
  scalable business model under uncertainty.
- Four contrasts sharpen the definition — small business (model is known),
  consulting (bespoke labor), lifestyle (outcome capped by design), research
  (goal is knowledge).
- Use the four-question diagnostic before applying startup tooling to anything.
- The rest of Foundations assumes you passed this test.
