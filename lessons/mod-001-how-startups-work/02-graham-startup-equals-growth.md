# Paul Graham — `Startup = Growth`

## Motivation

The definition from chapter 01 (a temporary organization searching for a
repeatable and scalable business model) tells you *what* a startup is. It does
not tell you what it should look like *while it's working*. That is what Paul
Graham's essay `Startup = Growth` (Y Combinator, September 2012) is for.

The essay is a shape reference — a single-lens view of the thing so that when
you're inside one, you can tell whether it is functioning. Read alongside Steve
Blank's process-oriented definition, it is the second half of the picture.

Read the primary essay. This chapter is scaffolding, not a substitute.

## The core claim

Graham's argument is a compressed one: a startup is a company designed to grow
fast. That is the single trait that separates startups from every other kind of
new business. Being newly founded doesn't make a company a startup. Being in
tech doesn't make it one. Growth rate does.

This aligns exactly with the chapter 01 definition — "scalable business model"
is a *pre-condition* for fast growth, and "repeatable business model" is the
mechanism by which growth continues. Blank's definition tells you *what* to look
for; Graham's tells you what it *feels like* when you've found it: a growth rate
that couldn't be sustained by a business that had to hand-craft each customer.

## Growth rate as the compass

The load-bearing consequence of `Startup = Growth` is that a startup should be
measured, week to week, on **growth rate**. Not revenue in absolute terms. Not
headcount. Not press coverage. Not fundraising. A weekly growth rate of a chosen
key metric — usually revenue or active users — is the compass by which every
other decision is oriented.

Graham argues this because:

- Growth rate is the trailing indicator that the search has found something. If
  the value proposition, the customer, the channel, and the pricing are all
  right, growth follows automatically. If any of them are wrong, growth
  stalls. Growth is the summary variable.
- Growth compounds. A startup growing 5% per week is a very different business
  in a year than one growing 1% per week — the difference is roughly 12x by
  annualization. Small differences in growth rate produce very large differences
  in outcome.
- Growth is diagnostic. When growth stalls or slows, something in the business
  model is wrong; the founder's job is to find and fix whichever assumption
  broke. When growth is strong, most other worries are noise and the founder's
  job is to protect the growth.

Graham gives a widely-referenced yardstick for what a healthy early-stage growth
rate looks like at Y Combinator: something like 5–7% week over week during the
early stage, with 10% being a strong signal and 1% being a warning sign. This
yardstick is the anchor Foundations returns to in mod-005 (`Reading the
Numbers`), where the founder-numbers packet includes weekly growth as a
first-class line item.

## The two objections most founders raise

Two objections come up nearly every time this framing is discussed. Both are
worth answering directly.

**Objection 1 — "But my business is complicated / enterprise / hardware / regulated;
I can't grow 5% a week."**

Graham anticipates this. The 5–7% number is a description of what YC-scale
software startups can plausibly do at the early stage, not a physical constant.
The load-bearing claim is not "5% per week" — it is "pick the metric that best
captures value delivered, and be moving that metric at a rate an investor would
call a startup." What that rate is depends on the market, the sales cycle, and
what "value delivered" means for the business.

An enterprise SaaS startup with 6-month sales cycles will not have a meaningful
week-over-week revenue number. It might instead track qualified pipeline growth,
signed logos, or deployed seats. The compass is the same; the metric on the
compass changes to fit reality.

**Objection 2 — "Focusing on growth encourages the wrong behavior (vanity metrics,
land-grab, unsustainable burn)."**

This is a real risk and Graham does not fully address it. In practice, growth
rate is the compass but not the only instrument. Foundations addresses this in
mod-005 by pairing growth rate with **default alive / default dead** — a
composite that asks whether current growth, current margin, and current burn are
consistent with reaching profitability before the money runs out. Growth without
that check produces companies that grow themselves into a wall.

The right framing is: growth rate answers *is the search working?* Default
alive/dead answers *will you survive long enough for the search to matter?* Both
are compass instruments; neither is optional.

## Growth as the disqualifier for non-startups

`Startup = Growth` is also a filter for chapter 01's four contrasts.

- A small business is not designed to grow fast. Restaurants do not 10x in a
  year — the cost curve won't let them. A restaurant that tried would break.
- A consulting project is not designed to grow fast. Doubling requires doubling
  the labor, and hiring at that rate cannot be sustained.
- A lifestyle business explicitly chooses not to grow fast because doing so
  would break the lifestyle.
- A research program grows on citation and rigor, not on customer acquisition;
  the metric of goodness is not a growth rate at all.

If your business cannot, even in principle, grow fast — because the cost
structure won't let it, or because the demand won't, or because you've chosen
not to — it isn't a startup, and applying startup instrumentation to it will
mislead you.

## The founder's leverage-point

The consequence of `Startup = Growth` for how a founder spends their week is
sharp: the highest-leverage question at any moment is *what is the growth rate,
and if it's not what we want, what is the single most likely reason it's not?*
Everything else — hiring, tooling, marketing, fundraising, product roadmap —
serves that question or it's a distraction.

This is why mod-004 (`Founder Operating Basics`) makes weekly measurement a core
part of the operating loop, and why mod-005 makes the growth number a
first-class founder-number. `Startup = Growth` is the reason those artifacts
exist in the shape they do.

## Summary

- Graham's `Startup = Growth` reframes the definition in one variable: a
  startup is a company designed to grow fast.
- Growth rate is the compass — it's the summary variable that tells you if the
  search from chapter 01 has found something worth building on.
- The 5–7% week-over-week yardstick is the anchor Foundations returns to in
  mod-005; different businesses will use different metrics but the same shape.
- Growth rate must be paired with a survival check (default alive / default
  dead) or it produces companies that grow into a wall.
- If your business cannot in principle grow fast, it isn't a startup —
  regardless of what its website says.
